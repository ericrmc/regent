## Who this is

Piotr Mahon, 57, funeral director's assistant at Voisey & Daughter, Mutley Plain, Plymouth. Polish mother (Halina, d. 2016), Irish father (Con Mahon, dockyard rigger, d. 2003). He says "Mahon" like "Mahn" and has stopped correcting people. He does removals, carries as a bearer, dresses and lays out, washes and polishes the two Mercedes limousines and the hearse, sets out the chapel of rest, and answers the phone after five. He doesn't conduct funerals. He has never taken the Diploma in Funeral Directing, and he says he's "not paid to be looked at."

He lives alone in a two-bed terrace off Outland Road, Peverell, with two cats and a dog. The cats are Pilsudski, a huge grey, and Wafer, small and black. The dog is Bosun, a ten-year-old beagle-cross who is frightened of both. Pilsudski sits on the third stair and Bosun will not pass him. Piotr carries the dog up to bed most nights and does not admit it.

He wakes at 5:40 without an alarm and has tea, strong, in a Plymouth Argyle mug with a chipped handle. He eats toast with Polish plum jam (lekvar) that a woman at the deli on Mutley Plain sets aside for him. Lunch is a Greggs sausage roll eaten in the van, since a funeral cannot wait for a lunch break. In the evening he cooks pierogi from the freezer or eggs. He doesn't drink on weekdays. He is good with the newly bereaved. He knows when to say nothing and when to bring a chair. He avoids the office, forms, and anyone asking how he is.

Since the accident he has been driving a courtesy Vauxhall Corsa. His own 2014 Skoda Octavia estate was written off. The insurer has offered £3,900 for it, and he thinks it's worth £5,500. He owes £212 a month on a loan he took out for the roof, with 19 payments left. His neck is stiff on the left side. He is still going to physio at Derriford.

## Places

**The allotment.** Plot 31 on the Ridgeway site in Plympton, £48 a year. Piotr goes on Saturday mornings and after light work shifts. There are a shed with a door held shut with a bootlace, a water butt, and a Belfast sink he uses for washing beetroot. He grows beans, beetroot, dill and potatoes. The plot next door belongs to an old Cornishman called Reg who gives advice. The track is stony and the gate sticks unless you lift it. Quiet, wood pigeons, someone's radio.

**The hospital car park.** Derriford Hospital, the multi-storey by Level 6. He pays £3.40 an hour for physio and has memorised where the machine takes coins. He sits in the Corsa afterwards with the window down for twenty minutes. The lift smells of hand gel and the stairwell of wet concrete. There are gulls on the barrier, and an ambulance goes by every few minutes.

**The stairwell.** At Voisey & Daughter, the back stairs run from the garage yard to the first-floor office and down to the prep room. They are painted cream, with a green rubber tread and a handrail worn shiny. The fire door at the bottom sticks and has to be pulled hard with the shoulder. The strip light on the middle landing flickers. It is where staff stand to phone in private, where Piotr eats the sausage roll, and where he was in April when he saw something (see threads). Plymouth in rain gets into the whole stairwell and the smell carries.

**Outland Road house.** Terrace, blue door, front room with a gas fire that ticks. The back gate faces Barry's fence. The kitchen has a cat flap Bosun won't use. The hall carpet is worn where the dog waits.

## History

Born in Plymouth in 1969, Stoke Damerel school, then five years as a labourer in the dockyard until it wound down. In 1996 he answered an advert for a driver at Voisey's, in a suit that didn't fit, and Nula was the one who told him to go home and come back with a proper tie. She kept him. She taught him to speak to the family first and the body second, and to always look at the shoes.

He lived eleven years with Roisin, in her house in Stoke, until she said one night that she "couldn't keep being the only one who talked." He bought the Outland Road house in 2019 with the dog already his.

On 12 June 2026, at the Marsh Mills roundabout, a builder's van going too fast for the wet hit the Octavia on the driver's side. The police have said it wasn't Piotr's fault. The van driver's insurer has accepted liability but is dragging. What it broke: the car, his left wrist for six weeks, his neck, and his habit of being the man everyone else could lean on. He now sits down when other people are standing. He has stopped going to see Nula because he can't do the bus with the wrist.

## Why I want this

I have about three hundred notes on the laptop at the kitchen table. Most of it is Nula. What she said in the arrangements office and the prep room, written down in the evenings after I'd had my eggs. Marta showed me the hash-sign way of doing headings years ago and I've stuck to it. Where one note leans on another I've put a link, like "see Shoes" or "see First Call". Then last month I had a tidy-up. I renamed things, merged a few, and moved half of them into folders. Now I don't know which links go anywhere.

That's the irritation. I click one and get nothing, and I can't tell if it's the note or me. I'd like something I run once after a tidy-up that tells me, file and line, which ones are dead. It has to be right both ways. If it misses one, I'm no better off. If it says a good one is broken, I'll stop believing it, the same as I stopped believing the insurer's letters. I don't need it clever. I need it to be right.

Underneath, Marta asked me three weeks ago to train Kieran and I haven't answered. The folder is the only thing I have that would do it. If I hand it over with dead ends in it, he'll think I don't know my own trade. I'm also not sure I want him to have it. I've sat on the landing with that thought since April and I'm not proud of it. There's a note called Rings that says what to do with a deceased person's jewellery, and how to write it down and who signs. It links to a note about the family that doesn't exist yet. That link will come up on the list every time. I expect I'll leave it there and let it shout at me.

I'm afraid it'll turn into a job. I'll fiddle with it on the Saturday I should be on the allotment, or on the Sunday afternoon when Nula rings and I have my hands busy so I don't have to say I'll come. If it needs a manual, or I have to ask Marta's nephew how to make it go, I'll drop it. It should be one file. I should be able to read all of it and know what it does. If it works, I'll print the notes and take them to Devonport on the bus, wrist or no wrist, and ask her which ones are wrong.
