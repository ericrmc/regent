Twenty to six, and the window over the sink had sun in it. Proper sun, low and yellow, lying across the lino and up the front of the cupboard door, and no warmth in it at all. I stood in it in my socks and it did nothing to my feet. Tea, thumb on the chip. The beer mat was still where it was. I have decided it is part of the floor now.

Bosun went out for once without being asked, and came back in wet to the elbows from the grass and stood shivering in the sun as if the two things had nothing to do with each other. Pilsudski watched him from the third stair. The parcel card was still on top of the fridge, and I rang the number on it standing at the counter with my other hand on the kettle. A girl said the potatoes had been out on the van Saturday and come back, and that they were at the depot at Marsh Mills, and I could collect with the card and some ID from eight till one. Or redelivery Wednesday. I said I would collect. She said, "Lovely," in a voice that had said it four hundred times since Friday.

I had the morning. Marta had said Saturday I could come in at two, there being only a small one tomorrow and nothing today but the phones and the hearse, which wants a wash after Mrs Tregear and the sleet. So I put on the good coat and the old shoes and took the bus down past the Plain. I was at the stop by the chemist at ten past eight. It is the one with the green bench and the ad for a cough syrup where somebody has drawn a moustache on the boy. Three of us stood in the sun with our coats done up. A man in a flat cap kept looking at the road, then at his watch, then the road. The chemist was putting out a stand of sun cream, in April, which seemed brave.

The depot is a shed the colour of a filing cabinet behind a tyre place. There was a hatch, a bell, and a lad in a fleece who took my card and my driving licence and went away for a long time. I could hear him moving things. He came back and said, "It's not here." I said the girl on the phone had said it was. He said it had been, and it had gone out again on the eight o'clock van, this morning, for redelivery, because it had been flagged, and if I'd rung at seven and said I was collecting it would have stayed, but the system didn't know. I said I had rung at six. He said the phones didn't start till seven. I said all right. He said, "It'll be with you by five, I'd think," and I said I'd be at work till five, and he shrugged in a not unkind way and wrote something on the back of my card and gave it back. It said Piotr M. Redeliv. PM. I put it in my pocket. There was nobody to be angry with, and I found I wasn't. It is a strange feeling and I did not trust it.

Because I was there, I went next door to the hardware place, since there is one. It is one of the old sort, all one long counter and drawers with brass handles, and a man in a brown coat, and the smell is paraffin and string and new galvanised buckets. I went in for a hinge screw for the cupboard. Ten minutes later I came out with a hinge screw and a roll of gaffer tape. Silver, two inches wide, £6.99. I do not know why. There is half a roll in the Octavia's boot, and another in the prep room drawer, and I had one in the shed at the plot last winter and could not find it. I had it in my hand at the till and thought, that'll be the very thing, for the washer seal, for anything, and the man said, "That's the good stuff, that," and I paid. On the pavement outside I stood looking at it in the light. It was sticky on the outside where the last shopper had picked at the edge. Seven pounds. I don't need it. I could hear my father, in the rigging shed, saying a thing that was mostly a noise. It sat in my pocket all the way back and knocked against my hip.

The bus back was late and full of schoolchildren on some sort of inset day, all in their own clothes, shouting. I got off at the chemist again and stood, because it was easier than the walk up Outland Road with the sun straight in my eyes.

There was an old man on the bench. Eighty, or more, in a good tweed jacket gone shiny at the elbows, with a carrier bag between his knees, one of those thin blue ones from the Co-op, and the bottom of it had gone. He was holding it up from the handles and it had opened along the seam, like a fish. There was a tin of soup in it, and a loaf, and half a dozen eggs in a box. He was not doing anything about it. He was looking at the bag with a sort of polite interest. I said, "That won't get you home." He said, "No, I don't think it will." He had a voice like a well-kept clock. I said, "Hold on," and I got the roll out of my pocket, and I have never been so glad of seven pounds. I knelt on the pavement, which my knee did not thank me for, and put the loaf and the eggs on the bench and laid the bag flat on the paving stones and taped it, three strips across the bottom and one down the seam and one all round like a bandage. Silver on blue. It looked like something for the space programme. He watched me the whole way through with his hands on his knees. "You've done that before," he said. I said, "Not to a bag." He said, "No, I meant the kneeling," and I looked up, and he was smiling, and I laughed out loud on the pavement in front of the chemist, with the chemist's girl looking out at us through the sun cream.

He wouldn't take it as a favour. He tried to give me a pound. I said no. He said fifty pence, then, and I said no, and he put the coin back in his pocket with a good deal of dignity and said, "Then I shall think well of you, and that's all I can afford." His bus came before mine. I held the bag from underneath while he got on, and he took it from me at the door in both arms, like a baby, and I stood there with the roll a bit lighter and stickier than it had been.

I went into the deli. I hadn't planned to. She was behind the counter with her sleeves pushed up and a tray of something with poppy seed, and she looked at me and said, "You came," and I said, "I'm a day late." It seems to be what I say now. She said, "Better than a week," and went into the back and came out with the jar, wrapped, tied with a bit of the string she uses for the pastries, so it looked like something for a christening. I said she shouldn't have troubled with the note. She said, "You don't say much, Mr Mahon, so I thought I'd say something." She has always said it Mahn. I have never asked her where she learned to. I gave her a twenty for a jar that is £4.20, and when she went for the change I said keep it, and she said, "For what?" and I said, "For the note." She looked at me a moment and put the change in the tin marked FOR THE SHELTER, and said, "Then it's theirs." I do not know what shelter. She didn't say and I didn't ask.

I took the card out of my jacket in the street, the blue one with the boat, and I did not write anything in it. There is a postbox at the corner by the bank. I stood at it with the card in my hand and I read the times of collection, Mon to Fri 5.30 pm, Sat 12, and I thought, she will get it Wednesday, or Thursday, and she will look at the postmark, and I posted it with nothing in it but my name. Just Piotr, in biro, in the little corner where the printed verse leaves a space. It went down into the dark with a soft flat sound. I stood with my hand on the cold red top. Then I thought that I ought to have put more, and that it was the wrong card, or that it was the right one and she would know, and I could not think which.

At work Marta was at the desk with the diary open and the phone under her chin. She raised a finger to me without looking. I waited. The hearse was in the yard, with the sun on the bonnet and a film of grey road on the sides, so I got the bucket, the leather, the good sponge. There is a certain pleasure in a car that has been dirty for two days. You can see the work. Kieran was there already, on the far side, doing the Mercedes with a chamois, whistling something. On the back window of the hearse, in what looked like shaving foam, somebody had written SORRY, and in smaller letters, APRIL FOOL, and a smiley. It wasn't a very good smiley. I said, "That yours?" and he said, "It was the day, wasn't it," and went pink. "It comes off," I said. "I know, I know, I was going to," he said, and did, with the chamois, in two quick strokes, and I did not say anything about the state of the letter S.

Marta put her head out of the back door. I said I'd need the Tuesday afternoon, the physio, at ten past two, and she said, "I know, it's in the diary, Piotr, I wrote it in myself." I said thank you. She stood there with her arms folded against the cold that the sun wasn't taking off the yard, and looked at the hearse, and at Kieran, and at me. "He's picking it up," she said. "He asked me what colour you like your tea." I said I liked it the colour it comes. She said, "That's what I told him," and went in.
