Twenty to six, and the sleet was on the glass like somebody throwing sugar at it. I lay and listened. The foghorn had gone in the night and left a hole where it had been. Bosun was across my shins. I turned my head on the pillow, left ear down, and the sleet went thin and far off, so I turned it back.

The washing machine was where I found the first trouble. It had stopped mid-cycle on Tuesday night and I'd left it, hoping. The drum was full of grey water with a skin of cat hair on it, and the door wouldn't open till I'd pulled the little tube out of the bottom and let it run into a washing-up bowl. It smelled of wet dog and something older. I knelt on the lino with the bowl and got the sheets out in armfuls, cold and heavy as a drowned man. The cupboard door swung open behind me, and the beer mat was on the floor again. I kicked it under the cooker.

There was a letter on the hall shelf that I hadn't been going to read. Long white envelope, insurer's name in the corner. It had come Monday. I'd put it by the phone book, face down, and every morning since I'd looked at the back of it, which is a clean white nothing with a flap. I took it out to the kitchen with the tea. Three thousand nine hundred pounds for the Octavia, in full and final settlement, and would I confirm in writing by the 28th. They'd put it in the second paragraph, quite low, in the same size print as the weather. The car is sitting in a yard at Ernesettle with its side pushed in. It's worth more than that, I know what it's worth, I've had it five years and every service is in the glovebox. I put the letter in my inside pocket with the hospital one and the biro from the bank. I wasn't going to answer it on the kitchen counter.

The funeral was at eleven, a Mr Leonard Hosking, eighty-one, forty years an electrician in the dockyard, wife Doreen and two sons and a grandson who was a bit too tall for the suit. Efford, and then the club in Stoke for the tea after. We were in the yard at eight doing the cars. Denny did the hearse with the chamois, and I did the Mercedes, with sleet turning to rain on the roof and the water running down the black in lines. You polish a wet car for the look of the thing. Marta came out with her clipboard and her good coat over her arm and said, "Are you all right on the left side? You're carrying high." I said I was fine. She looked at my shoulder and then at the car, and didn't say anything else.

Six of us at the door. Mr Hosking was heavy, oak with brass, and the wet on the handles made it a question of grip. We went down the path with the rain turning back to sleet halfway, small white grit blowing sideways into the mourners' faces, and Doreen in the front car with her hat in her lap. The Efford chapel has a smell of floor polish and lilies and the underneath of old radiators. The eldest son read something about a ship. He read it with his head down and his thumbs on the edges of the paper, and I looked at his shoes because I always do. Polished the night before, you could tell, and the left lace done in a double knot, the way a man does it who has been told all his life to be sure. The younger one's had a tear across the toe. That's a person who came straight from somewhere. I brought Doreen the chair when she stood too long, and she said, "Is it raining on you, love?" I said not much. She said, "It always did when he wanted it dry."

At the club the sausage rolls came round on a tray, and I stood by the door with my hands behind me and didn't have one. A man from the dockyard days, ninety if he was a day, took hold of my sleeve. "You're a Mahon," he said, saying it like Mayhon. "Con's boy. I was on the Ark Royal refit with him." I said I was. "Big lad. Never wore gloves." He held on to my sleeve a good while and I let him. When he went the cuff was damp where his hand had been.

My phone went in the yard at half past two. I'd stepped out to the stairwell for it, into the cream wall and the light going on and off, and a bright young woman from audiology said she was ringing to rearrange my appointment on the second of April, because the clinic that day had been "consolidated." She said it like something they'd done to an appliance. "We can offer the thirtieth of April. Half past nine, Level 3." I said the second was fine, I'd arranged a morning off. She said that clinic had been consolidated. I said, so I heard. There was a pause with a keyboard in it. "I can put you on the cancellation list," she said. "If something comes up, we'd ring you." I said I'd take the thirtieth, then, and she was very glad, and read it back to me. The strip light blinked through the whole of the reading. When she'd gone I stood with the Nokia against my chest and thought, four more weeks of turning my head on the pillow. I'd written the second on the calendar in the kitchen, in pen, above the pig. I would have to cross it out.

I went for the washing after work. There isn't a fault in the world you can fix at home once the machine has gone, and the man who'd come to look at it was in Brixham till the end of the month, according to his mother. So it was the launderette at the bottom of the Plain, the one with the yellow sign missing a letter, SUDS and a gap and then S. I took the sheets and the dog's blanket and two towels in a bin bag over my shoulder. It was half past five and the sleet had come back again, harder, out of a sky the colour of dishwater, running in the gutters as slush, stinging in the face.

There was a bicycle against the window, a woman's Raleigh, blue, with a basket. The back tyre sat flat on the kerb like something asleep. A lad in a delivery jacket was crouched next to it with his hands in his armpits and a look of having given up. He came in behind me and asked the room in general if anyone had a pump. The woman at the counter said no. Two students at the back didn't look up. I said I had a foot pump in the van.

I'd parked the van outside the chemist's. I went and got the pump from behind the seat and came back and he was already crouched again, and the valve was the thin sort, the kind that has a little brass nut on the end you have to unscrew, and my pump was made for the fat one. "It'll go," I said. "There's an adaptor." There wasn't an adaptor. I went through the pump and my pockets and the van's glovebox, and came back and knelt in the slush in my good trousers and pushed the head of the pump down on the valve at an angle and pumped with my foot. The air went out the sides with a hiss. "Mate," the lad said, "honestly, it's fine, I'll walk it." I said give me a minute. I pumped and the tyre didn't fill, and the sleet got into my collar at the back, on the cold side where the barber had cut it short. I said I'd done a hundred of these. I hadn't. He stood up. "Cheers," he said. "Honest. It's only up the road." He took the bike by the saddle and walked it away in the wet, tipped back on its front wheel like a dog on a lead, with the flat one dragging. I stayed there with the pump on my knee till a car went by and put a wave up the kerb over my shoe.

Inside it was warm and smelled of hot lint and blue powder. I put the sheets in the big machine, number six, which has a door as wide as a porthole, and fed the coins in. The woman at the counter had a service wash on a card, £9.50, they'd do it and fold it and I could pick it up in the morning. I said I'd do my own. "It's no trouble," she said. "I know it's not," I said. I sat on the bench by the window with the letter out on my knee, and the biro, and the paper on top of the hospital envelope to write on. Dear Sirs. Regarding claim number. I wrote the claim number. I looked at the drum going round with the sheets sticking to the glass and dropping away and sticking again.

Two men had come in after me and sat on the other bench with a bag of clothes each and no hurry. One of them was in a hi-vis jacket with the sleeves rolled up, sixty perhaps, with hands like shovels, and he talked in the tone of a man who has been asked a question at a dinner. "Forty years I did it. Bridges. Saltash and the others. You've got a hammer, a little one, and you go along the steel and you tap it. Every foot or so. And you listen. Doesn't matter what it looks like, you could have it painted like a bride. If the note's dull you know there's something hollow behind it. A void. You'd never see it with your eyes." The other man said, "And then what?" "And then you mark it with chalk and go on to the next," the first said. "That's all. Somebody else does the rest." He held his hand out flat and knocked on his own knee, twice. It made a sound like a knock on a door.

Somebody's machine buzzed, and it wasn't mine.

I wrote, I refuse to accept, and scratched it out. I wrote, I do not accept, and looked at that. On the road the sleet had gone to rain again, fat and quick on the glass, and the yellow sign made a wobbling light in the puddles. I folded the paper along its creases and put it back in my coat.

At seven I rang Nula from the step, under the awning, with the rain coming off the edge of it a foot from my shoes. Ten rings. I let it go eleven. I thought of the phone on its little table by the cactus, in the flat, and the three cards on the sill, and I thought of her getting up out of the chair one hip at a time and not making it. Or of her sitting there looking at it. The screen said Call ended. I thought of ringing the warden, and I didn't know the warden's number, and I went back in.

The sheets came out of the machine warm and heavy, and I put them in the dryer, and sat with my back against the wall of it while it turned. It shook a little with each rotation, a big steady tumble that you feel in your spine. The heat came through my coat. A dog blanket has a smell of its own when it's clean, still faintly of dog, like a room where someone's just left.
