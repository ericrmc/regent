Twenty to six, and the window was grey and wet, the frost gone in the night. It had left the sill dripping. Bosun was across my shins and Wafer was on my chest, which she does when the house has warmed up, a small black weight with her claws set in the blanket. I lay still and let her have it. The pipes were quiet. Down the stairs Pilsudski was on the third step with his tail hanging over the edge like a rope somebody had forgotten to coil. Bosun stopped on the fourth. I carried him past.

The new flask did its job. I filled it at six and left it on the side while I had toast, and at seven I unscrewed the red cap and the steam came up as if it had somewhere to be. I stood over it a while. It's ugly, the flask, the colour of a fire bucket, and it has a smell after all, faint, of new plastic and the shop. But it's hot. I don't know why I mind that it's hot and cheap and the other was good and dead.

The cupboard door was still on. I opened it and shut it, and it shut. I'd been meaning all morning to see if it hung true, and for that I wanted the spirit level, the long yellow one with the bubble gone slightly green, which has been at number 14 since October. Barry borrowed it for a shelf. I had thought about going round on Sunday and thought better of it, because it was Sunday, and because you don't knock for a spirit level on a Sunday when you've got a fence between you that neither of you has said anything about yet. So at ten to eight I went out the back and he was in his garden in a fleece with a mug, looking at the hedge as if it owed him something.

"Piotr," he said. He says it right. I'll give him that. "You'll be after the level."

I said no hurry, which was a lie, and he said it was in the shed somewhere, he'd dig it out, he'd bring it round tonight. He didn't move toward the shed. He turned his mug a quarter turn in his hands. Through the shed window behind him I could see something long and yellow leaning against a paint tin. I don't say I'm certain it was mine. It might have been another man's. But there was a smear of white across it that I recognised, from my own ceiling, from when I did the landing. I said tonight would be fine and went in.

The morning was Mrs Ackland from Efford, ninety-one, in the hospice, and her daughter in the corridor with a carrier bag holding the cardigan she wanted her in, and the lipstick. I've done a good many of those. You take the bag and don't say anything about it, and you look at the shoes. Hers were the brown lace-ups with a hole worn in the left one at the little toe, and I took them out of the bag and held them a while, and the daughter said, "She wouldn't get them mended," and I said, "No," and that was enough. I'd have said more if she'd needed it. I brought her a chair, and she sat on it with her hands round the carrier bag's handles, and I went in.

Marta was on the stairs when I came back, going up two at a time with the folder under her arm. She said, "Are you all right for Thursday? I had you down for the Penhale." I stopped. I'd forgotten the Penhale. I said I might be needed at Derriford Thursday morning, only for a check, and she said, "Right," and looked at me one beat longer than she needed, and went on up. I thought, well, that's said. Nobody said are you all right. She had already asked, in a way.

I'd got as far as the middle landing, under the light that flickers, when the office phone went upstairs, and after a minute Marta leaned over the rail and said it was for me. Audiology. A girl with a bright voice, not the one from January, said they were very sorry but Thursday's clinic had been cancelled, a consultant off with a chest, and could she offer me the second of April, ten to twelve. I stood with the receiver against my good ear and my bad ear against the cold wall of the stairwell and I said the second was fine. She said, "Lovely. And it's Mahone, is it?" and I said yes, and she said, "With an e," and I said I'd stopped minding. She laughed as if I had made a joke.

So it wasn't Thursday. I gave the receiver back to Marta, and she said, "Sorted?" and I said it had been moved, and she said, "Good, then you can do the Penhale," and it was so ordinary that I couldn't decide, on the way down, whether I was pleased. I'd been braced. You get ready to say a thing and then you don't need to, and the readiness has nowhere to go. I stood in the garage among the hearse cloths with my hands hanging, and it wasn't relief exactly.

The rota was on the corkboard by the wax and chamois, the week in Marta's square capitals, and I looked at Monday because of the vet. Bosun's check, they'd pencilled it, eleven o'clock, the woman at the desk in Peverell had said it before I could ask her for another time. Monday: 10.30 Trevarthen, at the crematorium, with a name against it that was mine. So eleven was out. Or the Trevarthen was short, a small one, and I'd be back for half past twelve. I stood and counted it on my fingers like a boy. I'd need to ring by Friday. I'd told her Friday. I took my pen out and didn't write on the board, because it isn't mine to write on. I thought I'd ask Marta, and then I thought I'd ask her tomorrow.

Lunch was the usual, on the step, the sausage roll gone limp in its paper. Then a quiet afternoon, the Mercedes, chamois in circles, nothing much. The bucket had gone the colour of tea. I did the wheel arches with the small brush. The hearse has a smell inside like a new handbag, leather and something floral, and I always put my head in a moment before I lock it. It's a private habit. I left at four.

I went to the garden centre on the ring road for compost, forty litres, for the seed trays. There's a lad there with a trolley who knows me now and says, "The beetroot man." I said the beans were first. I heaved the bag into the boot of the Octavia on its side, and at the seam, along the bottom edge, where the stitching had been done in some hurry, the whole thing opened, and black compost slid out over the boot mat in a long soft tongue. I stood there with the handles in my hands. It was still cold from the pallet, and it smelled the way it should, of wet bark and something faintly sweet, of a cellar. I said a word my mother wouldn't have liked. Then I scooped it back with my hands, a fistful at a time, into the two halves of the bag and folded the top over, and there was a dark line of it in the carpet that I'll be finding in June. The lad came over and said I could swap it, and I said it wasn't worth it, and he gave me another bag anyway, a ten-litre, and wouldn't take money. I don't know why that got to me.

Bosun was in the back, on the seat, with his chin on the headrest. I hadn't planned the towpath. But it was on the way, if you're generous about it, and the light was still up, so I parked at the end by the bins and let him down and we walked along by the water. The path there is packed cinder and puddles, and the creek was full and brown and flat, with two houseboats tied up on rusty rings, and a rope hanging slack off one of them, dripping. A heron stood on the mud. Bosun saw it and pretended not to. It's a place I've walked twice a year for twenty years, and I've never known the name of the pub on the far side, only the light in the window.

The sky went black. It came over from the west in about a minute, a dark lid, and I said to the dog, "Here we go," and then the hail arrived. It was properly hard, white pellets the size of peas, bouncing off the cinder and rattling on the roof of the houseboat like someone throwing gravel, and my hat was no use at all. Bosun went flat against my leg. I ran, or did what I do now that passes for running, to the boatyard hut at the bend where there's a bit of overhang and a window with a notice in it, and we crowded under. It stung. It came down for ninety seconds, I'd say, hardly more, and it was like standing inside a drum. Then it stopped, the way a tap stops, and the whole path was white and steaming a little and the heron hadn't moved.

I was standing against the glass. Someone had pinned a laminated sheet in the window, curled at the corners, with a coffee ring across the bottom, and I read it because it was there and I was under it. A ship under way gives way to a ship constrained by her draught, it said, because manoeuvrability and not right of way decides who can act. There was a little diagram under it of two arrows. Somebody had written in biro in the margin, in capitals, THIS MEANS YOU DEREK. I read it twice. Then Bosun leaned on me and I looked down, and he was shaking, and I put my hand on his head. His ears were wet through.

The hail lay on the path all the way back and then went to water. Some of it stayed in the grooves of my boots, little grey bits, and I let it.

At home the dog went straight to the hall carpet where he waits, on the worn place, and I could hear the cats somewhere above deciding not to come down. I took the compost through to the back and set the two bags against the wall by the door. It was six and there was no washing to be done, the washer still dead, though I'd looked it up in the book, and I stood and thought of a thing I'd read about a drive belt.

Supper was eggs. I did them in the small pan with a lump of butter, four, because I'd not eaten since the roll, and I couldn't be bothered with a plate, so I stood at the cooker and ate them out of the pan with a fork, with the grill light on and the extractor whining, and a heel of bread in my other hand. They were good. That's the thing. I stood there and wiped the pan with the bread, and looked out of the window at the dark square where the fence would be and, beyond it, at a light on in Barry's kitchen. I thought he'd be round with the level. I thought I might not go to the door. I could hear the gas fire ticking in the front room, where I'd left it on low, and I stood a while longer, with the fork in my hand, and Bosun's nails on the lino behind me, and then his nose against the back of my knee.
