# 2019-03-13

Twenty to six, and the fog was against the glass like a wet sheet. I couldn't see Barry's fence from the back bedroom, or the hedge, or the lamp on the lane. Nothing had a far side. I lay and listened to it. The foghorn out on the Sound was going, slow, and I could hear it with both ears or thought I could, and then I turned my head on the pillow and it went thin and one-sided. I turned it back. Bosun was on my feet and didn't stir.

The kitchen cupboard by the cooker was open again. It has been open since Christmas. The hinge on the bottom leaf has pulled out of the chipboard and the door hangs off the top one at an angle, like a man leaning on a wall. Every night I shut it and every morning it has swung out an inch and let the cold into the tins. I've been holding it with a folded beer mat wedged in the gap. The beer mat had fallen on the lino. I picked it up and put it back and pressed the door on it with the flat of my hand till it clicked, and it stayed, and I stood there with my palm on the cupboard for the time it took the kettle to start. The paint is cold and slightly tacky where the grease has got into it. I've been meaning to do the hinge for months. I've got the screws.

The radio was on low, Radio Devon, a gardening slot I don't listen to but don't turn off. A woman with a soft voice was going on about a man on Dartmoor with hives. I caught "loses her queen" and then the kettle rumbled over it. "...raises several at once, six, eight, and then the workers go through and kill the ones that are surplus, before they've..." The kettle clicked off and I lost the rest. She laughed at something. I poured the tea and by the time I'd got the milk in it was the travel news, and the A38 was slow.

The phone was on the side by the pig. The Nokia's the one I dropped on the ramp at Derriford in the autumn, and it has a crack across the top left corner, a fine white line like a hair, that has caught the edge of the screen so the first two letters of every text slide under it. I opened it to see the date. Wednesday the thirteenth. I stood and looked at it.

The twelfth was yesterday. Nula's. Every year of my working life I've sent her a card on the twelfth of March, with a robin on it if I could find one, because she said once that the robin was the only bird with the sense to come near a graveyard. Yesterday I'd gone round the barber's, and the thermos, and the hospital envelope, and eggs. I'd not thought of it once. I'd sat at the counter with the yolk on the bread, looking at the pig.

I didn't ring. It was ten to six. She's up by seven but she takes her time about the day, with those hips, and the warden comes at half past eight. I would ring from the yard.

The van wouldn't start at first. I turned it three times and the damp had got in it somewhere. When it caught, the fog was all round it in the yard light, and it was like driving into the inside of a cloud. Down Outland Road I could see the bonnet and the first parked car and a bit of white line. On the Plain the shopfronts came up out of nothing one at a time, each with a light in it, and went behind. I did twelve miles an hour. A bus went past so close and so slow it might have been a large animal. Nobody sounded a horn, because nobody could tell where it would land.

I was in the yard by seven and the fog hadn't moved. Denny had the hearse out with the door up and the chamois, and he looked like a man wiping a ghost. "No good doing it," he said, "it'll be wet again by nine." He did it all the same. I stood with the phone in my hand next to the Mercedes and rang her.

It rang seven times. It rang eight. I was going to put it down and then there was the click, the fumbling, the chair.

"Piotr," she said. She didn't say hello. She says my name like the first line of something. "You're up early or you're late."

"I'm sorry," I said. "It was yesterday. I never sent you a card. I forgot it, Nula, I've no excuse, I was..."

"You didn't forget," she said. "You remembered late. It's not the same. A person who forgets doesn't ring up in the yard with the fog on his voice."

I laughed. It came out of my nose. Behind me Denny's chamois went round and round on the roof of the hearse.

"I got two cards," she said. "One from the girl who does my feet. One from the man at the church. And a card from Alan, a proper one, with a printed message. I've put them on the sill next to the cactus. They look very well." There was the noise of the chair again. "Are you eating? You've got that voice. You had it in the winter your father..."

The line went. It didn't click. It just stopped, a small emptiness with a hiss in it, and the screen said Call ended in white letters, the first two gone under the crack. I stood with it against my good ear for a bit. Then I looked at the screen and put it in my pocket. I thought I'd ring back at dinnertime, and then I thought she'd have gone for her wash by then, and by the time I'd thought about it a third time the bell was going in the garage.

I did a removal at ten, a man in a flat at Stonehouse, eighty-something, on a bed with a hospital rail and a cardigan folded on the chair. His shoes were by the door, black and polished, with the tissue still in the toes. Somebody had been going to take him out. I didn't say that to the son. I brought the chair.

The thing I'd ordered came that morning or didn't. It was a new glass liner for Dad's thermos, from a firm in Sheffield that does them by post, nine pounds fifty and a fiver for the carriage. The email on the Monday had said Wednesday by 1 p.m. At half past twelve I rang the number on the email from the stairwell, with my back on the cream wall and the strip light going on and off above me like a slow blink. A boy said the parcel was showing as out for delivery. At two he said the same. At half past three he said, "It's showing as delivered, sir. Left in a safe place." I said I had no safe place, I had a front step and a blue door, and I'd been at work. He said he could raise a query. I said do that. He said it could take five working days. Somewhere under him a phone was ringing that he was not going to answer either.

I got home at ten to six with the fog down on the road like a lid. Nothing on the step. No card through the door. I looked under the wheelie bin, in the porch, round the side of the meter box. I went down the path and looked at the number on the door, the sideways shrug of it, and I thought, well, no wonder.

Barry was at my gate.

He'd come round the front, in the slippers he wears to the bins, a cardigan over his shirt and his hands hanging. He didn't have a letter. I thought he'd got the letter. I stood with my key in the door and felt my shoulders go up.

"That box," he said. "Yours has the card. The lad said he'd left a card."

"Oh. Yes." I got it from behind the pig and gave it to him through the door, and he held it out at arm's length to read it, because his glasses were in the other pocket. "Ah. Tuesday. I was at the hospital. Cataract, the left. They don't say you'll want somebody to hold your arm."

"I didn't know."

"No." He looked past me, down my hall, at the dog on the carpet and the cat on the stair. "I saw the thermos going in the van yesterday. On your back seat. With the dent. It's the vacuum, is it? It's gone?"

I hadn't thought he'd looked at anything of mine for years, apart from the fence.

"A degree a week."

"I've got a Stanley in the garage," he said. "The old green one. Pint and a half. Never had a tea in it that went cold, and I never took it anywhere. My Maureen used to say it had been on one picnic and didn't care for it." He stopped. He looked at his slippers. "I'll fetch it round. If you're wanting it. It's no use to me."

He was gone before I could say. I stood in the doorway in the fog with the wet coming in on my sleeves, and heard his gate, and his door, and then the long scrape of the garage, and I thought, there'll be a catch to it, and I couldn't think what.

He came back in four minutes with it under his arm like a loaf. It was heavier than Dad's. It had a dent of its own. I held it and it was cold all the way through, the way a good one is when it's empty, and I said thanks, and he said, "Right," and went back up the path with the fog folding in behind him.

I put it on the draining board next to the other one. Two of them. They looked like a pair in a shop.
