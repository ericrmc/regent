Twenty to six, and the window was shut and the glass dry for once. I put the kettle on and took Dad's thermos down from the shelf over the bread bin. It's the green Thermos with the dent in the shoulder where it went off a staging at Devonport before I was ten. I've filled it every working morning for years. I filled it again and screwed the cup on and stood it by the door with my keys, and Wafer came and sat next to it and looked at it as though it might be about to speak.

The knock came at ten past seven. That's too early for anyone but the milk, and we haven't had a milkman on this road since the nineties. A lad in a brown fleece stood on the step with a box under one arm and a handset in the other hand. Behind him his van was in the road with its hazards going, ticking orange on the wet.

"Coombe?" he said. "Fourteen?"

"Next one up. The one with the Rover."

"Right. Yeah. Only the number's worn off, isn't it, on yours, I thought it was a four." He looked at the door and back at his handset, and I looked at the door too. It's true. The screw has gone out of the nought and the metal one hangs by its top and reads like a sideways shrug. "I did knock up there. No answer. He wants a signature."

"I'll take it."

"He wants his own signature."

I held out my hands anyway. He gave me the box for a second, so I could see the label, and it was heavy for its size and something inside it shifted with a sound like a handful of nails. Mr B Coombe. There was no sender on it that I could see, only a barcode. Then he took it back, as if I'd been holding it under false pretences, wrote something on a card and put the card in my hand for Barry. He went off down the path saying, "Ta, mate, have a good one," and I stood with the card until his van had gone. I put it behind the pig. I'd been going to post it through Barry's door on the way out and I didn't.

There was a brown envelope on the mat as well, I'd stepped over it. It was the long kind, with a window, and the hospital's crest in the corner. I'd taken it for something else and left it. I put it inside my coat now without looking, to give it somewhere to be.

At Voisey's, two removals. A woman at a nursing home in Crownhill, and a man at Derriford who'd been on the ward since Thursday and had a wife who kept asking me whether he'd been cold. I said no. I said it in the level voice, and I put the blanket the way she'd have wanted it before we went. The mortuary yard at the back of the hospital is all concrete and roll doors, and a smell of bleach that goes up your nose and sits behind your eyes. I signed the book with the pen on a string, and the porter, a big soft lad named Callum who is always eating something, said the weather was going to turn.

It did. I'd left the van on Level 6, which I don't usually, because the ground floor was full, and I sat in it with the door open and the bag of my dinner on my knee. There wasn't any dinner. I'd forgotten. There was only the thermos. I unscrewed the cup and poured, and steam did not come. The tea was the temperature of a bath somebody had left. I took a mouthful anyway and it was flat and a bit metallic, a tea that had given up at some point around ten and hadn't liked to say. I tipped the cup back and shook the thermos next to my ear, and it sloshed the way a bottle does, without that heavy silence a good one keeps. The vacuum has been going since the winter, a degree a week, and I'd been telling myself it was the cold weather. I put my thumb over the dent. Dad drank from this on a girder over the Hamoaze in all weathers and it kept his tea hot till dark.

Then the hail came. It arrived all at once on the roof of the van, a hard white rattle, like being inside a drum somebody had thrown rice on. It bounced up off the concrete a foot high and ran along the gutters of the car park in little glassy rolls. A woman ran from a Fiesta to the stairwell with her handbag over her head, and a gull on the barrier rearranged itself and stayed put. I don't think it lasted ninety seconds. It was gone as it came, and the sun came out on the wet, and Level 6 steamed, and the gull shook itself and looked at me as if I'd done it.

I got the envelope out. Audiology. My name in a typeface that wasn't quite the right size for the window, so the P sat a little cut off, Iotr Mahon. Inside was the appointment, 2 April, 2.10, Level 3, follow the green line, and I thought, so it is a real thing. There was a leaflet folded in three. It said Please arrive fifteen minutes early, and Please bring somebody with you if you wish, and then a picture of an ear cut through like a shell. Three little bones. The hammer, the anvil and the stirrup, drawn very neat and grey. I sat with the leaflet on the wheel and read about the stirrup, which is the smallest bone in the body and the size of a grain of rice, and I thought of my mother's kitchen and the tiny stirrup in it somewhere, in the head of everyone who has ever talked at that table. I read it twice. I liked that they had named it after something you put your foot in.

The thermos went on the back seat next to the umbrella. Not in the bin. I didn't like to. It lay there rolling a little on the wet seat when I took the ramp down, with a slosh in it.

I had my hair cut at a quarter past five, because Marta had said, kindly enough, that I looked like a man who had been in a hedge. The barber's on the Plain is Turkish, two chairs, four photographs of footballers, a smell of talc and hot towels and something with lemon in it. Mehmet had a customer already, a lean man in a donkey jacket with his boots up on the rail and a cardboard tube under his arm, and I sat on the bench and waited under the radio. Mehmet said, "The usual? Short, not too short," and I said yes, and I let the cloth go round my neck.

The man in the donkey jacket was a carpenter. He talked to the mirror the whole time, not to anyone, in a slow West Country voice that took its time to get where it was going. It was a barn frame for somebody on the moor, oak, green oak, and I got some and lost the rest under the clippers. "You don't clamp it, see. You bore your hole through the mortice, and then you bore the tenon a hair off, and when you drive the pin it draws the joint up tight. A drawbore pin. The fastening and the clamping's one action." Mehmet said, "Ah," in the voice of a man who cuts hair. "One knock," the carpenter said, and tapped the rail with a knuckle. "And it can't be got out again without you break it."

Mehmet went down the back of my neck with the razor, on the left side, where it's stiffer than it ought to be, and I held my head very still. I could see the lot of it, my own face and a strip of grey, and the carpenter's boots, and beyond him the window with the wet Plain in it, a bus going by lit up like a fish tank. The radio was on a quiz. Somebody was being asked for a capital and took too long.

"You're quiet," Mehmet said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You are." He brushed my collar with the soft brush. "But today, a different quiet."

I don't know what that means. I paid him and gave him a pound over and he said, "See you in five weeks," which is what he says.

Home at ten to seven, with the back of my neck cold and light. Bosun came up stiff and pressed into my shin, and Pilsudski, on the third stair, looked at my haircut as though I'd been replaced. I didn't cook. I couldn't be bothered to lay a place. I fried two eggs, and I ate them at the counter standing up with the fork, the yolk going into the crust of the last bread, with the pig looking at me. The card from the delivery lad stuck out from behind it. It said COOMBE and a tick where a number should go. I turned it round so I could read the other side. Somebody had written 'Neighbour?' in biro and left the question mark hanging.
