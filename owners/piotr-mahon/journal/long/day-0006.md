Twenty to six, and the frost had gone soft. The garden was dark and dripping and the shed had come back out of it, a black box with the look a shed has in March when you haven't been near it. I made the tea and drank it at the sink. The Stanley stood on the draining board where I'd left it, and I put my palm on the side out of habit. Cold as a step. I filled it anyway for later.

The envelope was on the mat when I went through for the dog's lead. It had no stamp. It was cream, the kind that comes in a box of fifty, and on the front in biro it said Mr P. Mahon, with the Mahon written out very carefully, as if he'd practised it. I stood in the hall in my socks and read it under the bulb. Two sides, in the same biro. The gist was that a Mr Trewin, chartered, had been over the back boundary at Barry's request and had found the fence to be, and here he'd underlined it, eighteen inches over on his side. Not the hedge, the fence. Eighteen inches. He'd be glad to hear from me before any further steps were taken, and he remained, and so on. There was a phone number for Mr Trewin at the bottom in case I wished to contact him direct, which I took to be the part that was meant to frighten me.

I read it again for the number. Eighteen. I held my hands apart in the hall, a foot and a half, and looked at the gap between them. It is about the width of a dog lying down. Bosun looked at my hands and then at the door.

The salt is a pottery pig my mother had, with a hole in its back, and it lives on the worktop by the kettle. It's heavy, and if you lift it there's a ring on the Formica where it has stood for years. I put the letter under it, folded once. Wafer watched me do it. I didn't say anything to the pig.

It was a Saturday, so it was the plot. I loaded the Octavia in the half dark: the fork, the trug, the Stanley, and the bag of compost from the boot that had been thawing since Thursday. It was heavy now, heavier than frozen, the weight of a wet person. Bosun rode in the back on the old towel. Up the Ridgeway track the stones knocked under the tyres and the gate stuck as it always does until you lift it. Reg was there already, of course, in the flat cap, with his back to me and a flask on the water butt lid. The radio next door was on low, a man reading the racing, and the pigeons were going in the ash at the end of the site as if they'd been paid.

"Morning, Peter," Reg said. He has said Peter for eleven years and I've stopped that too. "You've brought your muck."

I hauled the bag out of the boot by its corners and it went. I felt it go before I heard it, a little give at the bottom seam, and then the whole belly of it opened and the compost came out on the gravel in one slow wet slide, black and hot-smelling, with a worm in it, pink and startled. I stood there holding an empty plastic sleeve. The heap came up to my boot.

"Cheap bag," Reg said. "They stitch them with cotton now."

He fetched the bucket and I fetched the trug and between us we shovelled it back with our hands, more or less, his knuckles the size of walnuts, his knee making him do it from a crouch with one leg out to the side like a man proposing. The stuff was warm in the middle, and I liked that. It was alive under the crust. I put my fingers in up to the second joint and it was near body heat, and I thought of the frozen one on Friday, and I didn't finish that either.

"Give me a hand with the fork," I said, and then, "Have you seen my long loppers?"

Reg looked at me sideways. "You lent them to Barry."

I had. October. For the top of the leylandii, which is a job for a man on a ladder with a saw and not loppers at all, and I had said, go on, take them, and he had said he'd have them back the same week. I could see them if I let myself, green handles, hanging on two nails in Barry's shed. It was strange to be wanting them. They're only loppers.

"Well, he's still got them," I said.

"Then you'll be wanting them back," Reg said, and topped up his tea from the flask cup and offered nothing else on the point.

I dug the bed for the beetroot. It's a job that pays you back in the arms. The soil came up in clods with the frost still in the undersides, and every clod had a smell like a cellar door, and I broke them with the back of the fork and threw the stones on the path. The compost went on in a black skin, and I raked it. Bosun lay against the shed wall in the one square yard of sun, with his eyes shut and his ears turning slightly to whatever the racing was doing.

The phone went in my coat, in the trug, under the Stanley, and by the time I'd found it the ring had stopped and started again. I went to the far end of the bed with my back to the radio and put it to the good ear.

"Mr Mahon? It's Sandra from Audiology at Derriford. Is that a good time?" I said it was. She said something about Tuesday and something about the clinic, and I said, sorry, could she say again, and she said it again, slower, and I said, sorry, and she went slower still and higher, in the voice they use for someone at a bus stop with a shopping bag. The clinic was off for Tuesday. A machine, or a member of staff, it went into the fizz. What she could offer was the second of April, ten past two. I said the second of April, ten past two, back to her like a man ordering, and she said that's it, and I said thank you, and she said, "You take care," and rang off.

I had no pen. I went to the shed, got my knee against the door and the bootlace off its nail, found a pencil stub in the seed tin and wrote on the card from my shirt pocket, under the Tuesday, in capitals: 2 APRIL 2.10. It looked like the writing on a parcel. The relief was so plain that I stood there a while in the smell of creosote and old string, with my hand on the door, and felt a bit of a fraud. Tuesday I'd been down for a removal after all. Now I wasn't going to need to say anything to Marta for three weeks.

By noon I'd done the bed and one trench for the potatoes and it was time to walk the dog off, so I locked the shed with the lace and took the lead. Reg said, "Mind the sky," and pointed with his flask to the west, where the cloud had gone the colour of a bruise.

The towpath is the way I go down from the Ridgeway on a Saturday when there's time, out of the estate and across the road and down through a gap in the fence, and then you're on the flat, the old cinder path with the water on your right. The tide was well out. The mud shone in long grey ribs and the boats on the moorings sat down in it, tilted, waiting to be lifted again. There were gulls on all of them. The smell was salt and rot and diesel and something clean under it, like a cut cucumber. Bosun went along slowly with his nose in the verge, reading. He was ten, and it showed in the way he set his back feet.

Two men were at the rail by the slipway where a steel boat with a red-lead patch on the bow was tied up. One was old and in a fleece with a hood, and the other was a lad with a fishing rod that had no line on it. The old man was telling him something with a finger. I went past at the pace of the dog, which is to say slowly enough to hear.

"No, see, you'd think it's whoever's on the right, everyone thinks that. It's not. A ship under way gives way to a ship constrained by her draught. Because she can't go anywhere, see. It's not who's got the right, it's who can move."

"Right," the lad said, and tried the reel.

"You go round her," the old man said. "Every time, you go round."

I'd gone by them. I heard the reel clicking behind me, a dry little ratchet, and then it was the water and the gulls.

The first hailstone hit the back of my hand like a thrown grain of rice. Then the path went white. It came across the estuary in a grey wall you could hear before it got to you, a hiss, and then it was on us, stinging, bouncing knee high off the cinders. I got my hood up. The noise on it was terrible on the left and a dull drumming on the right, and I found I was laughing, and I don't know why. Bosun tucked in against my legs and looked up at me with white on his eyebrows. I counted, because it's a habit of the trade to count when there's nothing to do with your hands, and by sixty I'd lost the top of the path under a crust of ice and by ninety it had stopped. Just like that. The sun came out low across the mud and every hailstone on the ground lit up at once, thousands of them, and began to go. The whole towpath was steaming, faintly. A halyard was ringing somewhere, and ten yards ahead a moorhen came out of the reeds and walked over the ice as if it were nothing.

I stood there a moment and then I turned round and went back the way I'd come. I hadn't meant to. The two at the rail had gone, and the steel boat sat by itself with the water dripping off her gunwale.

Home by two, and hungry in the ordinary way, the way of a man who's been digging. I put four eggs in the pan with butter and did them soft and put two slices of bread in, and I didn't lay a place. I ate at the worktop with the pan in one hand and the fork in the other, standing, with my elbow next to the pig. The eggs were good. The bread was the end of the loaf and the crust was hard. I mopped the pan with it. Pilsudski came in and sat on the mat and looked at the eggs and then at me, and Bosun, who was under the table, made himself very small.

When I'd finished I stood with the pan in the sink and the tap running and looked at the pig. The letter was a white corner under it. I didn't lift it. I thought, I could measure it. It's a fence, it's a straight line, there'll be a post I know is mine, and a tape from the shed, and I'd know by teatime. I thought about that for the length of a kettle. Then I thought about Barry standing at the far side with his own tape, watching me, and Mr Trewin's number on the sheet, and the loppers on the nails.

Outside the hail had gone from the shed roof already and the light through the window was thin and clean, a kind of white that showed up every smear on the glass. I dried my hands. The tape was in the drawer with the string and the batteries and my father's flask, where I'd left it, and I took it out and ran a few inches of it out over my knuckles and let it snap back in, and it made a noise like a small door.
