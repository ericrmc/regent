# 2019-03-06

Twenty to six again, and a clear head again, which two days running makes me suspicious. Tea in the Argyle mug. The window over the sink showed the garden the whole way down, every slat of the shed, the water butt with a lid of ice on it. Frost on the lawn thick enough to leave a print. The sky was that hard pale colour that means it will be a good day for looking at things and no use for anything else.

The cupboard door was still on the worktop where I left it. I pressed a thumbnail against one of the matchsticks, gently, and it held, and I pressed harder and it still held, but it hasn't had its twenty-four hours. It's been twenty-two. I took my hand away. The shelf inside was bare to the room again, the tins and the foil and the good saucepan, and Wafer sat in front of it as if it were a shop window. I gave her breakfast from the top of the cooker, which meant reaching over the ring and dropping the biscuits in from a height, and she looked at me as if I had done that on purpose to be difficult.

Bosun came down the hall and stopped at the third stair. Pilsudski was on it, a grey lump with his eyes shut, and the dog stood with one paw lifted and waited to be told it was all right. I said it was all right. He didn't believe me. He went the long way round through the front room and into the kitchen, along the skirting, with his belly low.

The compost was still in the blue crate in the hall, split side to the wall. I had said tonight, the allotment, after work. Reg had said on Sunday that he'd be up there Wednesday or Thursday with his barrow and I could put my beds in with his help, forking it in, since the ground would be dry enough for once. It was going to be Wednesday and then it was going to be Thursday. Today is Wednesday. I know I had it down as Thursday. I had the beans in my head, a row along the north side where the ground stays damp, and the dill that comes up wherever it likes.

For an hour I did nothing much. I washed the mug. I looked at the letter under the salt without lifting the salt. Then a noise started next door and I stopped looking at the salt.

It wasn't the trimmer this time. It was a hammer, and it was on the fence. Not a wild hammering, slow, regular, one blow and a pause and another, like a man driving a nail and thinking between each. I stood at the back door with the door open and the cold coming in round my ankles and I could hear it clearly across the frozen garden. Tock. Then nothing. Then tock. Through the bare gap where the hedge had been I could see the top of his head and a hand with the hammer in it, moving along the panels on his side. I couldn't tell what he was fixing. He might have been putting something up. He might have been knocking it back into line. If it's the line, it's the line he says it is, and there's a nail in it now.

I shut the door. The kitchen was quiet, and then I heard it through the wall anyway, softer, in the pipes almost. I put the radio on. It was a man talking about the price of something. I turned it off.

The vet rang at ten to eight, before I'd even got my shoes on. The same girl. She said, "Mr Mahn, sorry, it's Chloe from the surgery, we got cut off," and I said I remembered, and she said she'd got the wrong end of it yesterday anyway, that the vet could do the check Monday at eleven or the following Tuesday, first thing, and which did I want. I said Monday. She said, "Lovely," and I heard her typing. Then I said, no, hold on, and told her I hadn't looked at the rota. She said no worries, she'd pencil it in and I could ring by Friday if it didn't suit, and that there was a chance he'd want to book a scale and polish, and did the dog eat all right. I said he ate everything. That was true. He ate a sock in 2017. She laughed, a real laugh, and I felt better about her than I had. Monday at eleven. It might be a funeral. Marta doesn't put Monday's list out until Friday.

I put the dog's lead on the hook and did not take him. Not because of the frost. It was a day for a walk, the kind where you can see all the way to Cornwall, but I had the compost on my mind, I'd decided, and I wasn't going to leave it split in the hall. I got the roll of black bin bags from under the sink and doubled two of them and lifted the whole bag from the crate into them, holding the split shut with my forearm like a man carrying a bad drunk. It weighed what fifty litres weighs. A little ran off down my sleeve and into my shirt, cold, and it smelled of leaf and, under that, faintly, of something a bit sour, like a shed. I tied the neck with a strip cut off a third bag. It looked like a body. I stood it upright in the crate and it slumped forward, and I thought, well, that's not a thing to say out loud, and I didn't.

Then I put it in the boot of the Octavia. The back seat was down for the ladder. It rolled against the wheel arch when I shut the door and settled with a soft push, like something sighing.

It was a bright sharp drive in. The sun was low on the left and got in under the visor and made everything on the dash go gold, the dust, a hair, the crumbs from Monday's sausage roll. The Plain was full of shadows with edges. The woman from the deli was rolling down her awning and waved a pair of tongs at me. I waved. I didn't stop, and I wondered if she would keep the jar aside for me tomorrow, whether it works like that or only if I go in.

At Voisey's, Marta was in the yard already, coat on, phone in her hand, and she said, "Piotr, Mrs Rudd's cancelled," and I said, "Cancelled?" and she said the family had rung at seven, the daughter, she'd got a date from the crematorium she liked better, Friday, and the viewing we'd set for this afternoon was off. No, not off, moved. She said that with her eyebrows. I'd got the chapel to set. I had been going to do it after the cars. Candles, the little table, the kneeler nobody kneels on, the good lamp instead of the ceiling light. I'd have been doing it all morning at my own pace, patient and quiet, and it would have been nice.

"Sorry," said Marta. "I know you'd got yourself into it."

"It's all right," I said.

She waited as if I might say something else. I looked at the hearse. There was a thin scum of frost on the roof that had not gone in the shade. I said, "You'll want the cars for Friday, then," and she said yes, and thanks, and that was it. I did not ask about Alan or the man in the blue car who came Tuesday. I noticed I didn't ask, the way you notice a tooth.

So the morning went to the cars anyway, and then the prep room, which wanted a proper clean. That is my favourite job and I would not say it to anyone. The steel, the drain, the tiled walls with the white grout, the smell of the disinfectant that gets in your nose and takes the whole day's talk out of it. I ran the hose. The water went down the drain with a small hollow sound that I've heard for twenty-odd years. I did the trolleys' wheels with a toothbrush. I put my head against the cold of the cabinet for a moment, no reason, just because it was cold. Then I straightened up.

The sausage roll on the landing. The light ticking. Somebody had left a mug on the rail and a ring of tea at the bottom of it had gone brown and dry.

At two my phone went, a number I didn't know, and it was Reg's daughter, the one in Saltash, and she said he'd asked her to ring, he had been meaning to but his phone wasn't working properly, and he was very sorry but he couldn't do this evening. His knee. He had it up on a stool and the doctor had said. She said, "He says the ground's too hard anyway, Mr Mahon, and it'll keep," and I said "Mahn," and she said, "Sorry?" and I said never mind, and to give him my best and tell him to keep it up, the knee, and she said she would. She said it as if I had said something funny.

So the compost stayed in the boot. I thought about whether to go up on my own. It would take an hour to get there and in this light I'd have forty minutes of it. I could have dug a bit, put the bag by the shed, and had the gate to lift. But the ground would be iron. I'd have to sit in the shed with the bootlace and drink from the flask. I think I would have been all right with that, actually, more than I like to say. But the thought of the bag on its own in the boot, sliding, made me sit in the van at the back of the yard and do nothing for ten minutes.

Home by half five. Sun behind the roofs and the sky going a clear green above them, and a cold that came straight up through my soles. The bag was still in the boot when I stood looking in. I didn't move it. Barry's hammer had stopped. From the top of the garden the fence looked exactly as it had. I couldn't see a new nail anywhere, and I ran my hand along the panel at shoulder height, where the wood was rough and frozen under my glove, feeling for what he'd done, and I got as far as the third post and found a small bright head in the wood that wasn't rusted, and I stopped there with my hand on it, and out of the bare gap where the hedge had been I could hear his kettle
