# 2019-03-11

Twenty to six, and I'd been awake since three. Not the whole time. I'd go under and come up again with the rain loud on the back bedroom window, and every time I came up I was in the middle of the same sum. Eighteen inches. I'd stretched my arms across the dark to see what it was, and it was about the width of the dog, who was on my feet. Carried up. Not that I say so.

My legs had their own opinion of yesterday. I'd walked the Dawes home from the bottom of the hill with the chain looped over the crossbar, and it took the best part of two hours and I'd not brought the pump, the tape or a coat that wasn't too warm. The bent link had gone in the shed drawer, on top of the string. It wouldn't close. I'd tried it with the pliers till my thumb went white and then put the pliers down and looked at the wall.

Tea at the sink, standing. The window was shut again. Cold had come back in the night, as Barry said it would, and the glass was running on the inside. The pig stood by the kettle with its ring round it. I didn't lift it. Wafer watched me not lifting it from the top of the fridge.

By the time I got the van out of the yard at Voisey's the rain was properly down. It wasn't the hail kind. It was the kind that comes straight and heavy and lasts, with a noise like gravel poured on a tin roof. I'd done two removals by ten, a man at Derriford and a woman at a house in Efford where the daughter held the banister the whole time we were on the stairs, and I'd said the level things, and brought the chair, and looked at the shoes. Hers were slippers, tartan, with the heel trodden down. I always look. It tells you what the person thought was coming.

Back at the branch Marta was at the front desk with the phone against her shoulder and a pen in her teeth. She held up a finger, then a sheet of paper, and put the sheet down on the counter in front of me while she said, "Yes, Thursday's fine, and I'll bring the hymn sheet round myself." It was the leave form. The yellow one, from the pad, with boxes. Alan had got a new pad in January and everyone had taken to it the way you take to a stranger in the church.

"Every appointment that's in working hours," she said when she'd put the phone down, and she took the pen out of her mouth. "He wants them on paper now. I'm not the one who's asking."

"No."

"Have you got anything coming?"

I'd not said a word about it. The card was in my shirt pocket under the Tuesday. I took it out and turned it to the light. 2 APRIL 2.10, in pencil, in capitals, like a parcel.

"Ears," I said.

She looked at me a moment longer than she needed. She knows how to leave a space. I don't know whether she learned it off Nula or whether it was always there, but I've noticed she doesn't fill it when I say things like that. "Right," she said. "Put the time and put where. There's a box for reason. You can just put medical."

I wrote it. My hand was not good with the biro, and the pen kept skipping on the carbon, so that the 2 looked like a swan. Under Reason I wrote Medical and then, because it looked bare, Audiology, and then I crossed that out and it looked worse. Marta didn't take it off me. She turned it round with one finger to read it and put it in the tray with the others.

"Ten past two," she said. "I'll put someone on to cover the afternoon."

"Tuesday," I said.

"Is it?" She looked at her own desk diary. "So it is. Tuesday." She wrote it down. She made a small mark, a tick or it may have been a line through something. I'd have liked to see which. On Saturday I'd been relieved that I wouldn't have to say anything for three weeks, and now it was Monday and I'd said it, and I was the same man as before, except that a girl I'd taught to fold a sheet knew I couldn't hear out of one side.

The lunch was the sausage roll, from the Greggs on the corner, in its paper bag gone see-through. I took it to the stairwell as I do. That's where you go, the middle landing, under the strip light that flickers, with your back to the cream wall. The rain had reached it. It comes down the stairwell somehow whatever you do, through the vent and the gap under the fire door, and the whole place smelled of wet concrete and a bit of the prep room coming up, the cold clean smell of it, and floor polish. The handrail was damp under my hand. The green rubber on the treads had a shine of wet where people had come in.

There is a windowsill on that landing, deep, with a frosted pane over it that doesn't open, and on the sill a tin. Huntley & Palmers, the old blue and gold, with a picture on the lid of some ladies in a carriage, worn down to the metal at the corners. It's been there longer than I have. Nula had it before me and I'd say her boss had it before her. It's full of screws. Brass ones for the furniture, the handles and the plates, and steel ones, and cup hooks, and washers in a twist of newspaper, and a lot of things nobody has a name for any more. I had one handle to do that afternoon, the second from the left on an oak veneer that had come in Friday, and the screw that had come with it was a sixteenth too short. I'd noticed on Saturday morning. I'd come to the tin now on purpose, and eaten the roll as an excuse.

I put the bag down and got the lid off. It went with a rasp of tin on tin. The tin was heavy, heavier than it looked, and the noise inside was a dull bright pouring when I tilted it, a little tide of small metal. I put two fingers in. That's something I like, honestly. Screws in a tin have got a coldness on top and a warmth further down, where your hand has been. I ran them over my palm one at a time. Brass, no. Brass, too fat. A black one with a slot as wide as a coin. A screw so small it might have come off a watch. It felt like reading, only with the wrong sense.

Somebody had the radio on in the garage, turned up to beat the rain, and the voice came up the well thin and level, a man on Radio Devon with a long sentence in him about the moor. I got some of it and lost some. "A catchment's response is measured as lag between rainfall and peak flow, which tells you about the ground and not the rain." Then the rain came harder and covered the next bit and he went on into something about the Tavy and I couldn't have said what. I stood with a screw in my palm, holding it up to the flicker. I'd have liked to hear the rest. I don't know that it was for me.

The door at the top opened and it was Denny from the garage, wet to the shoulders, in his overalls with the collar up, and he said, "Mother of God, have you seen the yard?" and went past me down the stairs with his own sandwich in a bag. I heard him shoulder the fire door at the bottom. It sticks, it always sticks, and you have to pull it and lean, and I heard it give, and the noise of the wet came in for a second, and then went off.

I went to have a look, because it was there. You can't see out of the frosted pane but the top light above is clear, and if you go up on the bottom stair of the next flight you can see the yard. It was empty. The Mercedes had a sheet over it. The hearse was in its bay, gleaming, with rain bouncing off it a foot in the air. Beyond the gate the Plain was a grey river. Not a soul. A man's umbrella was rolling along the kerb on its own in the wind. It went all the way to the corner and stopped against the bus stop, and nobody came out to fetch it. Every shop door along was shut, or had someone standing back in it, and I stood on the stair and watched until my roll had gone cold. I thought, that's what it looks like when everyone has been told. A street with everyone gone in and the doors shut and only the water about its business.

I don't know how long I was there. Upstairs I could hear a chair go back and Alan's voice, low, through the wall, and a second voice I didn't know, a man's, easy, with the vowels of somewhere further up the country than here. They laughed, both together, the way men do at a number. The door closed on it. I looked at the tin on the sill, lid off. I hadn't got the screw. I found it after another minute: the right one, a thirty-two, half-inch. Brass, a bit dulled, in a corner where it had been waiting under a cup hook. I held it against the hole in the handle plate I had in my pocket and it went in as if it had been asked for.

I put the lid back on. It doesn't fit as it should, the lid, it never has, one side sits proud, and I pressed down with the heel of my hand until it went with a small dry crack, and then I stood a moment longer with my hand flat on the picture of the ladies in the carriage.

The rain hadn't slackened at four. The gutters were roaring on the back of the building and the yard drain had given up and stood a hand deep. I took the van round and left it at the kerb and went in the front for once, the length of the shop with my shoes squeaking, and out again with the umbrella that had come to rest at the bus stop, because nobody had fetched it, and it was a good umbrella, black, with a wooden handle. I don't know why. Someone would want it. I put it on the back seat.

At home Bosun was on the worn place in the hall carpet with his chin on his paws, and he came up slow and stiff in the hindquarters and pressed himself into my legs. The dark had come early with the rain. Pilsudski was on the third stair. I stood in the hall in my wet coat with the dog against my shin, looking at the cat, and the cat looked at me, and neither of us was going to be the one to give way, and I thought of the old man at the rail with his finger. It's not who's got the right, it's who can move. I took my coat off. I went and lifted the dog.
