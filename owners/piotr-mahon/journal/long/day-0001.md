# 2019-03-04

Slept badly. Woke at ten past two with my heart going for no reason and lay listening to the gutter, then again at four, and at 5:40 I was up anyway, so the night counted for nothing. Tea in the Argyle mug. Stood at the kitchen window with it and could see nothing but my own face and the kettle behind it.

The cupboard by the cooker was open again. It has been open since I moved in. The hinge on the bottom has pulled out of the chipboard and the door hangs off the top one like a dropped jaw. I push it shut and it stays for about an hour and then it swings out an inch. Cat food, foil, the good saucepan, the torch. I have folded a beer mat under the door twice and both times a cat has taken it out. Wafer, I think. Pilsudski wouldn't bother.

The letter was on the table where I left it on Saturday, under the salt. I hadn't put it in the drawer because then it would be in the drawer. Cream envelope, Barry's handwriting on the front, careful, all capitals, like a man filling in a form for the police. Inside, one sheet. It says he has had a surveyor, a Mr Trewin, look at the rear boundary "as a courtesy," and that Mr Trewin's view is the fence is a foot and a half over on Barry's side at the far end. It says he would prefer to settle this "as neighbours" and that he is "happy to discuss." Then a line at the bottom that says I may wish to take my own advice. I read it again standing up. I still don't know what a courtesy is when it comes with a surveyor.

The fence isn't mine, in the sense that I didn't put it up. It was there when I bought the house in the spring. Larch-lap panels, gone green at the bottom, posts that lean like people at a bus stop. The hedge is his and it is the one that's grown, six feet out and leaning on the panels so they bow. If the fence is a foot and a half over then the hedge is on my side by more than that, and I don't think he's thought of that. Or he has and that's why the letter is so polite.

I wanted to look at the line. It was still dark, so I went for the torch. I got it out of the cupboard, and it was heavy in the wrong way. Thumbed the switch and nothing. Unscrewed the end and both batteries were furred over with white crust, like something you'd find on a pipe, and one of them had leaked a brown stain into the spring. The smell was sharp, like a coin in the mouth. I held it under the tap and then thought better of that and wiped it with kitchen roll. There is a drawer of batteries somewhere. I know there is. It wasn't the drawer I opened.

So I did what I do. I took the dog out.

It was just getting light, and the rain had started properly, not the Plymouth kind that hangs about but the kind that comes off the roofs in sheets. Bosun stood in the hall by the worn place in the carpet and looked at me. Pilsudski was on the third stair with his paws tucked, watching him do it. Bosun will not pass that cat. I said, "It's only water, boy," which was a lie, and he came out to the step and stopped and looked back at the door as if I'd done it on purpose. I put his coat on him. He hates the coat. I hate the coat.

I could have gone round the block. I went down Outland Road and along to Central Park and round the pitches, and then I kept going, down towards Pennycomequick, under the bridge where the water was coming off the road in a brown skin, and on. By the time I was on the Hoe side there was nobody about at all. The street had been emptied out, no cars moving, a bin bag going along the gutter by itself. The rain came sideways off the Sound and got in my collar, and my neck was wet through by the time I realised how far I'd gone. I was not thinking about the fence. I was thinking about the fence the whole way, if that makes sense. I was walking so as not to knock on Barry's door. If I had stayed in I would have gone down the garden and stood at the panel with my hand on it, and then I would have gone round and rung his bell, and I know how that would have gone. He would have said "Piotr" the way he says it, both syllables like coins, and then I'd have said something I couldn't take back. So, three miles in the rain. The dog trotting with his head down and his ears streaming.

On the way back a man in a doorway on Mutley Plain, sheltering, said, "You've got a wet dog there," and I said, "He's got a wet man," and neither of us laughed.

Bosun shook himself in the hall and got the wallpaper. I towelled him on the mat and he leaned into me, all his weight, the way he does when he's decided it's over. Then I went and stood in the kitchen in wet socks. The cupboard door had swung open. I shut it. It swung open. I looked at it for a while.

Half eight. I had the morning free because we had nothing until a removal at half one, and Marta's rota said so, and I hadn't argued. Toast with the plum jam. The woman at the deli gave me the jar on Friday and said, "Don't tell the others," as if there are others. I put too much on. I ate at the table with the letter next to the plate, not touching it.

Batteries. That was the errand, and I made it the errand. The Post Office on the Plain sells them on a card by the till, so I went in there, and I have never seen it so full. The rain had done it. Everyone with a parcel or a form had come in out of the wet at once and stood dripping on the lino in a line that went back to the birthday cards. I took a card of double A's off the rack and got in behind a woman with a pushchair and a bag of something in bubble wrap. Ahead of her a man was trying to send a tyre. A tyre, in a bag, to an address in Cardiff. The girl at the counter was very patient. She said, "It's over the size, my love," and he said, "It's a tyre. It's round. It's the same size as a tyre." I stood there twenty minutes with the packet in my hand. The line moved one person. Behind me a woman kept saying to nobody, "Well, this is ridiculous," in a voice that wanted someone to agree with her, and I did not, and I could feel her looking at the back of my head.

Water ran off my sleeve onto the floor. Everyone's shoes left the same grey print. I've always looked at shoes; Nula told me to. You can tell most things. The pushchair woman had trainers gone at the toe. The man with the tyre had good brogues, resoled. I thought, he will pay for that tyre one way or another. I don't know what I meant by it.

When I got to the counter I said, "Just these," and the girl said, "Is that all?" and I heard myself say, "Do you do a copy of anything? Like a plan?" and she said no, that was the library or the Land Registry, and I said fine. I paid £3.29. Something about the price. It seemed a lot to have waited for.

Home. In the kitchen I put the new batteries in the torch, and I had to scrape the corrosion from the contact with the back of a teaspoon first, and it came off in a grey powder that went on my thumb and wouldn't wash. The torch worked. A good white beam. I turned it on the ceiling, which was stupid, in daylight, and it made a small yellow coin over the cooker.

The rain had eased to something you could stand in. I went out the back in my work shoes and the good coat, which is stupid too, and walked down the garden with the torch, and put the light into the hedge, where the fence post goes into the roots. It's a mess in there. Brown needles, a crisp packet, the post rotted at the base with a green collar on it. I got the tape from the shed, the old rigger's tape my father had, with the brass hook end, and I tried to measure from the back wall of my house to the fence at the near end and again at the far. The numbers were different. That is all I've got. A tape isn't a surveyor. The hedge dripped down my neck the whole time.

At the top of the garden I could see Barry's kitchen light on and the shape of him at the window, still, not doing anything. I don't know how long he'd been there. I lifted the torch, as if to wave. I don't know why. I turned it off before he could tell whether I had.

Then I went in and did not write back. I put the letter under the salt again. If I answer it that's me agreeing there's something to answer.

At one I put on the black suit and the tie Nula would pass and drove in to Voisey's. Marta was on the phone at the desk with her hand over the mouthpiece, and she mouthed "Derriford, Ward 9" at me and pointed at the keys. That was all she said. Mr Keast, eighty-six. I took the van. The lift at the hospital smelled of hand gel, and the porter, who knows me, said, "Wet enough for you?" I said I'd been in worse, and pushed the trolley in, and for the next hour I didn't think about the fence at all, which I suppose is what the job is for.

But in the van afterwards, with the sausage roll going cold on the dash, I found I had the torch in my coat pocket. I had carried it all day. It was still warm from the pocket, or I thought it was. I took it out and thumbed the switch, and it came on, and I sat there in the daylight shining it at the footwell, and then I thought, what did Con say about a boundary, something he said about rigging, that a line's only as true as the man who hauls on it, and I couldn't get the rest of it, and I sat with the light on.
