Ten past three, and again at ten to five, and by the time twenty to six came round, the hour I usually get for free, I had been lying awake so long the rain had stopped being noise and turned into the shape of the room. It had come in about two. First the gutter at the back, the bad length over Barry's side that spits, then the whole roof. Bosun was pressed along my shins and I could feel him not sleeping either. Wafer had gone somewhere. Pilsudski, when I came down, was on the third stair with his eyes open, and I carried the dog past him, and neither of us said anything about it.

Tea. The thumb on the chip. I had the toast and the jam but I couldn't have told you what it tasted of, only that it was sweet and there was a lot of it.

Before I left I did what I'd promised myself last night with the belt half round the pulley and my knees gone. I put the washer on empty, a short cycle, and stood in front of it with my arms folded like a man at a bus stop. The drum turned. It went round with the low wet grumble it used to have, the water came in, and at the spin there was one thump, a single one, and then it settled. I stood there through all of it and then through the pause after. I didn't touch it. If you're pleased too soon it goes wrong. I turned off the tap at the wall and went to work with the knowledge sitting in my chest and no one to give it to.

The rain was properly down by half past seven. It wasn't the hail of Tuesday, which was at least an argument. This was flat and grey and total, coming off the roofs in sheets, and by the time I turned into Mutley Plain the street had emptied as if a whistle had gone. Nobody. A bus with its lights on and nothing in it. A man's newspaper in the gutter turning to porridge. The awning of the deli was pouring off its edge in a rope, and the woman inside had her hands flat on the counter and looked out at me as I went by, and I raised two fingers off the wheel and she raised her chin. Nothing said. Nothing needed.

Marta had left a form on the first-floor desk under the stapler, one of the pink-and-white removal sheets, for the Ackland lady from Tuesday, which I had not filled in because I had told myself the daughter had been in the corridor and it could wait. It could not, said the sticky note, in the square capitals. Time of call, time of removal, from where, by whom, and then the box at the bottom for property. I sat in the chair with the pen and it was cold in there, the office heater ticking and not warming anything. I wrote the times. I wrote Hospice, ward side, and my name. Then I sat over the property box for longer than there was any need. She'd had the carrier bag, the cardigan, the lipstick, the brown lace-ups with the hole at the little toe. I wrote it all down in the small hand I use for it. The pen ran dry on the word lipstick and I had to go over it. I signed. My signature goes uphill on that desk. Marta's door was shut and I could hear her voice through it on the phone, low, the one she uses with the crematorium, and I put the sheet on her blotter and didn't knock.

That was the paperwork. It should have been nothing, and I carried it about with me the rest of the morning like a stone in a shoe.

The stairwell was cold and wet-smelling, the way it goes in a real downpour. I don't know how the rain gets in, there isn't a window that opens. But it climbs the concrete in the walls, and the whole flight smells of a wet dog that has been dead a while. The green rubber treads were dark with our boots. The light on the middle landing was doing its flicker, three quick and a long, and I stood under it because I'd come out with nothing in my hands and wanted a reason to be somewhere.

That was when I saw the bracket. The lowest stanchion of the handrail, the one by the fire door, had worked loose. I'd felt it wobble on Tuesday and again on Wednesday with my shoulder against the door, and this morning there was a gap between the plate and the wall you could put a finger in. One screw was out and lying on the tread, and the other had backed halfway. I picked it up. It was a brass one, a slot head, the slot mangled by somebody's bad screwdriver forty years ago.

On the shelf on the middle landing, under the fire notice, there's a biscuit tin, a Huntley & Palmers, a Christmas one, with a coach and horses on the lid gone pale from hands. Nobody knows whose it is. It has been there all my time. It's full of screws. Not tidily. Screws and brass hooks and cup washers and a few odd keys, and the tin has the noise of a shaken cash box. I took it down and the weight of it surprised me, as it always does. I sat on the third step, with the tin between my knees, and tipped a good handful out on the tread beside me and started to find one that matched.

It took a long time. That was the pleasure of it, though I'd not have said. Everything was nearly right. A brass one, but too short. A long one with a round head. Two the right length and wrong thread, which you can tell by the way they bite and then don't. My fingers went through them by themselves. I could hear the rain on the yard door and behind it a drain gargling. Somewhere below in the prep room the radio was on, Terry's, on Radio Devon with the volume too low, and the door was propped with a bucket, so that a man's voice came up the stairs in pieces. He was talking to somebody about the river, the Tavy or the Plym, some catchment, and I heard him say, in the careful voice of a man who's said it before, that the lag between the rain and the peak flow tells you about the ground and not the rain. Then the presenter said, "Right, and we'll come back to that," and there was a jingle, and then a woman selling conservatories.

I found one. Number ten, not quite as long as the old, with a head like a button. I stood, my neck pulling on the left the way it does when I bend and rise too quickly, and I held the bracket to the wall and put the tip of the screwdriver into the slot, and turned. It bit. I put my weight on the rail after and it held. It didn't move at all. I ran my hand up the wood, shiny and worn hollow where a hundred and something years of palms have gone, and it was cold and slightly damp, as if the rail had been out in it.

I didn't put the tin back straight away. I sat there and looked at the mess of screws on the tread and thought I ought to sort them by size, into the egg box from the kitchen or something, and that I never would.

At eleven, the rain went on. I had a removal to do from a nursing home in Compton, nothing difficult, an old man, and Terry drove, and we sat in the van at the kerb for ten minutes waiting for the rain to lift because neither of us wanted to be the one who ran. The wipers beat. The whole of the street was under water at the drain, a brown lake across the road with a crisp packet turning slowly in it. There was no one out. The nursing home had its lights on in every window in the middle of the morning. Terry said, "I never seen it empty like this," and I said no, and that was the whole of the conversation, and I'd not have wanted more of it.

Inside they were kind and short of staff. The matron had a tea towel over her shoulder. The man lay with his mouth slightly open and a look of mild surprise, and I closed it, and held my hand there a moment until it stayed. His slippers were tartan, and they'd put them together neatly at the foot of the bed with the heels touching. I looked at the shoes. It's the habit. They're always the truest thing.

At one I ate the sausage roll on the middle landing and the tin was still there beside me with the screws piled on the step, and someone came down from the office, Marta, and stepped over them, and said, "You've had a good clear-out," and I said the rail was loose. She put her hand on it. She said, "That's been going since I started," and I said, "It's done now," and she went down and pulled the fire door with her shoulder, and it stuck, and she had to do it twice.

The vet. I had said Friday. I took my phone out at the top of the flight, and the signal there is a rumour, so I went halfway down to the landing and stood under the flickering light with my good ear to it. It rang six times. A girl said, "Peverell Vets," and I said it was Mahon about Bosun's check on Monday, that I had a funeral at half past ten and could I move it, and she said, "Oh, hold on a sec, I'll just," and there was a click and a hum, and then a sound like a shell held to the ear, and the line went. I looked at the screen. One bar and then none. I stood with it against my head a moment more, and the rain came down the wall, and the light did its three and a long. I put it back in my pocket. I'd try again in a minute, from the yard maybe. I didn't go to the yard.

The afternoon was thin, as I'd hoped. I washed the second Mercedes with the doors open to air out the smell of wet felt, and dried it with the chamois, and my arm ached, and the whole time the rain kept on lightly and the water ran off the roof of the car in a curtain I had to work behind. Dennis Pym telephoned the office, I heard, to ask about a bearer's rota, and Marta said, "He's here, do you want a word?" and I found something to do at the far end of the garage.

Home by five. Bosun was on the hall carpet as if he'd been carved there. The cats had gone through the house in a wave. The washer, when I opened the door and looked at the drum, had a little water standing in the rubber lip, and nothing else. Nothing had come loose. I put a small load in, two shirts and a towel, and shut the door and watched it through the round window, the towel going over and over. I don't say I trust it.

Eggs again. It seems all I do. I had them on toast this time, sitting down. The gas fire was ticking. The rain had thinned to a fine drift at the window, and there was still nobody in the street.

Barry came at half past seven. I heard the gate before the knock, and the gate swore the way it does, and he stood on the step under the porch light with his hood up, holding the long yellow level in both hands like something he'd been asked to carry into a church. "There," he said. "Found it. It was in the shed all along." He held it out. I took it. It was cold and slightly damp, and the bubble in its little window had gone as green as before. Across the top edge, near the end, there was a smear of white paint, dry, the shape of a thumb. My landing ceiling. Neither of us looked at it. I looked at his face, and it was a face like anyone's, pink and wet from the rain, and he said, "Sorry it's took so long," and I said it was no trouble.

He stayed a second longer on the step than he needed. He looked past me down the hall, at the dog, at the worn place in the carpet, and I thought he was going to say something about the hedge. Then he said, "Wet enough for you?" and I said it was, and he went, and the gate swore again behind him.

I took the level through to the kitchen and stood it against the cupboard door, the one I'd hung, and laid it flat along the top edge, and bent to watch the bubble.
