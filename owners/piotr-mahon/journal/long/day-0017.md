Twenty to six, and the window was open an inch where I'd left it on Tuesday to let the damp out. What came through it wasn't cold. It smelled of wet earth and someone's toast, and there was a blackbird going in the sycamore two gardens down as if it had been kept waiting. Bosun was across my shins. Wafer was on the sill with her back to me and her tail moving slowly, looking at the bird in the way that means nothing will come of it. I lay there and did not want to get up, and I did not want to lie there either. That was the whole of the mood, and it lasted all day.

Pilsudski was on the third stair. Bosun stopped on the fourth. I carried him.

The kitchen was warm before I'd lit anything. I stood with my palm on the cooker as I did on Monday, out of habit, and it was only the temperature of a hand. Tea, and the thumb on the chip. Toast with the plum jam, and the last of the lemon drizzle Lorraine brought round, which I had meant to save. It is a good cake, sharp, with the sugar crust that cracks. I ate two pieces standing up and then washed her plate and dried it and put it on the table by the door, so I would not walk out without it. The level has been at number 14 since October. The plate has been at mine since last night. I thought about that and did not like what it said about which of us was the better neighbour.

The Penhale was at half past ten at Weston Mill, a big turnout, a man of eighty-eight with sons who had come from Bristol and Reading and stood in the car park with their coats over their arms, red in the face, saying it was a lovely day for it, and then looking as if they wished they hadn't. It is a poor thing to say at a funeral. But everyone says it when the sun comes out. The coffin was pale oak with his cap on the lid, a flat tweed one, and I took the left front corner. My shoulder knew the weight before I did. Six of us, the slow step on the gravel, and the smell of cut grass from somewhere, which isn't right in March but was there. I kept my eyes on the back of Terry's collar. There was a wasp, or something that wanted to be a wasp, going round the wreath on the lid, and one of the sons watched it the whole way in and did not brush it off. I liked him for that.

Marta did the committal. She has a voice for it, low and even, that carries to the back without ever rising, and I stood at the side with my hands folded and my suit jacket sticking to the back of my shirt. I hadn't sweated in a suit since September. By the end I was counting the minutes, which I don't do. I tried to stop.

Afterwards the family went into the tea room, and I went round the back and undid my tie and stood in the sun against the brick. Warm brick. I hadn't leaned on anything warm for months. The whole wall was giving it back, and I stayed there until Terry came round with a cigarette and said, "You've gone quiet, Pete," and I said I was always quiet, and he said, "Quieter," and I said it was the sun. He nodded as if that was a reasonable answer. Nobody else calls me Pete. I've stopped minding that too.

I was meant to be back at the yard by one. Marta had said, on the way out to the cars, that I could take my hour late if I wanted it, since the afternoon was thin, and I said I would. There's a market on Thursdays by the church on the hill, twenty stalls, and I had it in mind that I would walk through it and buy nothing. I put my jacket over my shoulder like a man who had somewhere else to be. I had not thought of asking her about Monday and the vet. I saw her go off in the car with the phone at her ear and I thought, tomorrow. Well. I've said tomorrow twice now.

The market was busy in a shy way, as everyone was, blinking. There was the fish stall with its bed of wet ice and the smell of it, cold and iodine and a little sweet, and a woman in a green fleece sluicing the boards. There was the veg man with the leeks in a bucket, and a stall of cut flowers in zinc buckets, daffodils with their heads still tight. There was a man with hats. A man frying onions on a flat plate for a hot dog stand, so that the smell came at you first from thirty yards, before you could see it, and made you say yes to something you hadn't decided on. I bought one. I stood with the paper going translucent in my hand and the fried onions burning the roof of my mouth, and I was glad of it, more glad than a hot dog deserves.

There was a bicycle leaning against the side of the veg man's barrow. It was a black one with a basket on the front and a dynamo lamp, an old delivery sort, the frame heavy as a gate. The back tyre was soft. I could see it from where I stood, the way the rubber had sagged at the bottom and the rim sat low. I don't know why I noticed it so hard. I put my thumb on it in passing and it gave like dough. The veg man was weighing sprouts for a woman and said without looking, "That's the nephew's. Been like that since Tuesday. He's gone off with the keys to the lock and left it there for me to look at." I said I had a pump in the car. It was a foot pump, a green one, that lives in the boot under the compost stain and that I bought for a bike I no longer own. He said, "Go on then," as though it was I who wanted it done, and I went and got it.

I knelt on the cobbles in my funeral trousers. You can't do that at fifty. I did it, one hand on the barrow, and pushed the pump down and felt the resistance come up under my foot, and the tyre took the air with the hard little hiss of a good valve, and the rubber came up round and firm. I'd done about thirty when I heard the other hiss. It was faint, from the stem, not from the tyre, and when I put my ear to it, it was a steady thin sound like a kettle a long way off. I said, "It's the valve, or the tube." The veg man said, "It'll see him to the bank," and I said it wouldn't see him to the top of the hill. He shrugged. I let the pump lie a while. Then I stood up, and my knee made a noise, and I looked at the basket on the front with a bundle of newspaper in it and a single leek, and I thought I was going to say something else, and didn't.

Beyond the flowers there was a table of second-hand books, paperbacks in crates, and two men at it in anoraks, with a book between them, one reading aloud to the other in a flat voice as if giving directions. I stopped because there was a gap at the table and I was on the wrong side of the crates. "The bird is flown at a weight," he read, "and the weight is the control, because a bird that is not hungry does not return." The other man said, "Well, there you are, then." The first one turned the book over and looked at the back of it, and said it was two pounds, and the other said he'd only wanted to see. Then they moved off toward the hat man, and I stood at the crates and didn't pick up anything.

What I found, and this was the good part, though I'd have called it luck if you'd asked me, was at the end of the row. A man with a trestle table and a heap of belts and vacuum bags and little bags of screws, mostly for machines whose names had gone off the market years before. I'd nothing in my hand but the pump and the hot dog paper. I said I had a washing machine, the drum wouldn't turn and the motor whirred. I had looked up the drive belt on Monday and written the number off the plate on the back, in biro, on my hand, and it had come partway off with the washing-up. He took my wrist. He turned it to the light and read what was left. "Nine-ish-nine, E, is it? Or a three." I said I thought a three. He went through a box without looking, with the kind of fingers that know, and came out with a belt in a paper sleeve. He said, "That'll be six pound. If it don't go, you bring it back, and I'm here Thursdays, and Saturdays Torpoint." I gave him a five and a one. He didn't put it in a bag. It sat in my jacket pocket the rest of the day, coiled in its sleeve, with a faint rubbery smell that came out when I sat down.

I drove back with the window down and the hot dog going sour in my mouth. I was restless. I had to sit with my hands on the wheel at the lights on Mutley Plain, and the traffic was the ordinary kind, a bus, a van, a woman with a pushchair, and I wanted it to be going somewhere different. I turned on the radio and turned it off. I thought of Devonport, of the bus over the bridge, the smell of the top deck in the sun, and then I turned right where I should have turned left, and had to go round the block.

The afternoon was the hearse, the hearse cloths, the small brush on the wheel arches. It was so warm in the yard that the chamois dried before I'd finished a panel, and left a bloom, and I did it twice. The stairwell was warm as well, which was strange, the stone giving off a dry stale heat, and for once it did not smell of rain. I sat on the middle landing under the light that flickers and did not eat anything. Someone was on the phone above me, low, saying "I know, I know," and I got up and went down and pulled the fire door with my shoulder.

I put Lorraine's plate on the front step of number 14 at half past five, under a saucer, with a note, because I could see through the glass that the hall was dark. I wrote, Thank you, very good, P. I stood on the step a moment with the sun on the back of my neck, and I could hear a radio somewhere in the back. Then I went round by the side and Barry was out at the bottom of the garden with the hedge trimmer, going along the top of the leylandii with the cable trailing behind him across the lawn, and he saw me across the fence and switched it off. In the quiet the drone of it went on in my ears. He said, "Level!" like a man remembering his own name. "It's at my brother's. I took it to do his shelf and it's still in the boot, I think. Or it's in the shed. Friday, Piotr. Definitely Friday." He turned the trimmer a quarter turn in his hand, the way he turns his mug. I said Friday was fine. There was a white smear across the shed window, the shape of a long thing leaning, but the sun was in the glass and I couldn't have sworn to it.

I went in. Bosun was on the hall carpet. I took my jacket off and hung it, and the belt fell out of the pocket, and I picked it up from the mat and stood holding it a while, running it through my fingers, a black loop with a ribbed inside, warm from me. Then I took the back off the washer with the screwdriver, on my knees on the lino, with a torch in my teeth and the cats watching from the doorway, both of them, in a row, with the same face.
