Twenty to six, and the frost had got onto the outside of the kitchen window in ferns. The first I've seen since February. I stood and looked at it with the tea going cold under my thumb. It was clear behind the frost, the sky the colour of the inside of a shell, and the light came in flat and hard and showed up every smear on the glass. I had meant to wash it. I have meant to wash it since the autumn.

The bucket was under the machine where I'd put it last night. A green plastic one, the sort a child takes to the beach, with a crack down one side the length of my hand. I had it from a man at the boat yard at the end of the towpath, where the road stops. There was a hull up on trestles and a smell of pitch and old rope, and he was scraping at it with a tool like a chisel bent the wrong way. I asked if he had anything I could put under a machine. He looked at me as if it was a reasonable thing to ask a boat, went into the cabin and came out with this. "Bring it back when you're done," he said. I said Thursday. He said, "No hurry," and went back to scraping. There was a cupful of brown water in it this morning, with a skin of grey on top, and I stood and looked at it and knew that if I gave it back on Thursday I would have nothing to put under the door. I emptied it down the sink and put it back. I've had it one night and it already feels like mine, which is not a good thing to notice in yourself.

Marta had said the funeral was at two, a Mrs Lisle at Weston Mill, and that I wasn't needed till twelve. So I did what I'd not done for years. I put the towel in the boot, under the bucket, and drove up to Burrator.

I hadn't decided about the water. I'd have said I was going for the air. But you don't put a towel in a boot for the air.

The road up from Yelverton was empty. There was frost in the shade of the walls and none in the sun, so the whole way was stripes, light and dark, light and dark, like a slow film. I left the car by the dam. There was one other, a white van with a ladder on it and nobody in it. I walked the path round to the east side, the tarmac one, along the edge of the trees. My breath went in front of me. The water was perfectly still and the whole hill stood upside down in it, every rock, every dead bracken stalk, and a heron on the far shore that didn't move the whole time I was looking, so that I began to think it was a post.

There is a place past the second bench where the bank goes down in a shelf of gravel, and I remembered it from a Sunday with my father, who sat on a rock in his good trousers and smoked and would not go near the edge. My mother went in to her knees in her slip. I was nine. I have thought of that all my life as an embarrassment, and standing on the gravel this morning with the sun on the back of my neck it seemed a marvel. A woman of forty in a slip, in Dartmoor, in April.

I took my shoes off. I took everything off, quickly, the way you take a plaster off. My clothes went on the rock in a heap and the tie on top, and I stood there in the light with my whole body gone to gooseflesh and my neck stiffer with the cold, and looked at the water. It was clear to the bottom for the first yard, the gravel gold, and then it went dark green and then black. A ring of ice, thin as a fingernail, was lying against the stones at the edge and it broke when I put my foot down with a noise like a small glass.

I went in fast. That is the only way. To the knee, the thigh, and then a stop, because everything stops, the breath stops, your heart stops trying to be a heart, and then you go under because you have decided to. I did not think of anything. For a count of about four there was nothing in the world but the cold, an enormous flat white noise. Then I came up and the air hit my face and I made a sound I have only ever heard from the dog, a long shaking hoo, and swam six or seven strokes out with my chin up, and turned. The sun was on the top of the dam. I could see my clothes on the rock, small and dark. The heron had gone.

I can't say how long. Two minutes. I was out and standing on the gravel with the water running off me and my skin the colour of boiled ham, and it was only then, with my feet going numb on the stones, that I thought of the towel. It was in the boot, under the bucket, a quarter of a mile away, at the other end of the path. I said something, aloud, and a wood pigeon went up out of the trees.

I dried myself on my shirt. It is not a thing I recommend. I put my trousers on wet-legged and my jacket on over nothing and my socks in my pocket and my shoes on bare, and my tie I could not find. I went along the bank on my hands and knees for a bit. I looked in the pockets, in the shoes. It was nowhere, and I thought, well, it's in the water, and then, no, I had it on the heap, and then I saw it, on the gravel, under the rock, where I'd knocked it. So it was not lost, only where I'd left it.

In my jacket pocket, when I put my hand in for the car key, there was a slip of paper, soft as a leaf from being sat on, folded twice. Hardware, Marsh Mills, Monday. One hinge screw 0.60, one gaffer tape 2in silver 6.99, total 7.59, cash 10.00, change 2.41. Thank you for shopping locally. I had not thrown it away. I don't know why. I never keep them. I keep nothing for the tax, I have never claimed a thing. I stood in the road by the dam with my hair dripping down my collar and read it as though there might be more on the back. There wasn't. I put it in the pocket again. There is a drawer in the kitchen with a good many of these in it that I have not looked at since Roisin's day, and I know that, and I put it back anyway, and said I would look at it later.

The man in the van with the ladder had come back. He was eating a pasty on the bonnet in the sun with his boots crossed. He looked at me, at the wet hair, the collar, the shoes with no socks. "You never," he said. I said I had. "Was it cold?" I said it was. He nodded slowly, as if I'd given him something to think about, and took another bite. "I always mean to," he said. "Then I don't." I said it was mostly a matter of not thinking. "That's the trouble," he said. "I always am."

I sat in the Octavia with the heater going and the window open a hand's width. The whole of me was buzzing, a good buzz, a clean one, as if I'd been washed in something stronger than water. And under it, like a stone under a mattress, the phone. Yesterday's call. The man's voice behind her, the shape of a question. I got it out of my pocket and looked at it. Nothing. No message, no missed call, only the time, 9.52, and the picture of the path. I put it face down on the seat. I thought, I could ring now, she will be up, and say sorry about the line, it went. And I thought about what she'd say, and how she'd say Piotr, with the door opening a crack, and what would be behind the door. I did not ring. I drove down with the window open and the wind on my wet head.

At the yard Kieran was already in with the kettle on. He had done the Mercedes and it stood with all its chrome shining, and he was polishing the number plate with the corner of a cloth, more carefully than it needs. I went past him to the prep room and stood a minute in the smell of it, the cold tiles, the soap, the bleach, the faint sweetness behind it that you do not name. It is the one room where I am never distracted. Mrs Lisle was in her own dress, a blue one, with a brooch. I looked at her shoes first, the way Nula said. Sensible, with a strap, worn down on the outside of the left heel. Nobody had polished them. I did it, with a soft cloth and the tin from the drawer, and I found I was humming, which I stopped.

Marta came down with the paperwork and stood at the door with her arms folded. "Your hair's wet," she said. I said I'd washed it. "At eleven in the morning." I said it was a long story. "Right," she said, and there was a look, the little look she gives that I have never been able to read, and she went back up. The stairs took her weight slowly. I heard the fire door stick and give.

The funeral was as they are. Fourteen at the chapel, a granddaughter in a hat too big for her, the organ a bit flat in the middle. I carried at the front left. My neck did not complain, and I noticed that and was almost sorry, having got used to being told. Kieran, opposite, kept his eyes on the back of the priest's cassock the whole way in, and stepped exactly on the beat, which he can't have been taught. I thought, he will be good. It did not make me glad.

On the way home I thought there might be a card on the mat, and there was a bill from the water people, a flyer for a man who cuts trees, and no card of mine, of course, that was going the other way. Wednesday, or Thursday, I had said. She would look at the postmark. I stood in the hall with the flyers in one hand while Bosun leaned against my shins and Pilsudski watched from the third stair, and I thought, the bucket.

I took it out from under the machine and looked at the crack. I could mend it. I had gaffer tape, silver, two inches, and that would take two minutes. It would hold, I think.
