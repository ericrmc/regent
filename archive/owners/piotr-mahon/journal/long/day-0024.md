Twenty to six, and the window was open an inch. I hadn't done that. It must have been Wafer, or the latch has gone the way of the cupboard door. Air came in soft, smelling of cut grass from somewhere, though nobody cuts grass in March. Bosun was on my shins and had kicked the blanket off, which he never does. Pilsudski was on the third stair, stretched out flat with his belly showing like a rug. I carried the dog down past him and Pilsudski opened one yellow eye and shut it again. I think that was contempt too, but a lazy sort.

Tea, thumb on the chip. Dry toast at the back door. The garden was wet and steaming a little where the sun caught the top of Barry's fence, and there was a blackbird on the washing line going at it as if it had been paid. I stood in my socks on the step. I could not remember the last time I had stood there and not seen my breath.

The machine in the hall was still blinking. I pressed it with the toast in my other hand. It was the vet's girl, very bright. "Hello, Mr Mahon, just confirming Monday at eleven with Miss Adeyemi for Bosun, and we've put it on the home number, so if you can't do it just give us a ring." Then a pause, and she said it all again, the whole thing, as if the tape had asked her to. So they had used the home number after all. I wiped the crumbs off my fingers onto my trousers and stood looking at the pig on the shelf. Monday at eleven and the Trewin at ten. I thought, I'll ring at nine when they open. I thought it the way you think you'll do a thing when you have no intention of doing it before nine.

I went out with no overcoat. It felt like leaving the house without my trousers. By the crossroads I had my jacket over my arm and by the Plain I had my sleeves rolled, and every window was open and every person was walking slowly with their face turned up, like plants. A man outside the bookmaker's had taken his shoes off. The deli had its door propped with a crate and there was a smell of coffee and something with cinnamon. The woman was not in the window. I went by. I was aware of going by.

A ten o'clock at the crematorium, a man of seventy-eight, a big turnout, all of them too hot in their black. I could see it on the backs of the necks. The women fanned themselves with the orders of service, and the little paper flapping sounded like a flock of something. Dennis and Kieran and I and two of the man's own nephews took the weight. Dennis breathed like a bellows going up the steps and said, low, "Nice bit of weather for it," which is what he says whatever the weather. Then in the porch he took my hand in both of his and said, "Piotr, Piotr, how are you keeping," and I said I was keeping, and he said, "Good man," and looked past me for someone else. Hot hands. Dry and hot.

We were back by half eleven. Marta was on the phone in the office again with the diary open and her hand flat on it, and she saw me at the door and mouthed, market, and held up a finger, then two, and pointed at her own throat. I took it to mean flowers for Friday, the two dozen white she wants from the woman on the corner stall, and something about string. I went down the back stairs into the yard. The Mercedes were shining and had a bloom of dust on them already, the first this year.

So it was the market on a Thursday. I walked it, since parking is a lie. Down past the Guildhall with my jacket over my shoulder, and in under the roof where it is dim and cool and the whole place smells of frying onions, old paper and wet cardboard. Every stall had its front pulled out to the edge of the aisle. Somebody was playing a radio, a man reading out racing, and somebody's child was screaming for the pleasure of hearing it come back off the girders.

The flower woman had the whites. She wrapped them in brown paper and said, "You're Voisey's, aren't you, the tall one who doesn't say," and I said I supposed I was. "My mum went through you in 'ninety-eight," she said. "You did her hair." I said I did not remember. "You did," she said. "You brushed it forward, and my auntie said she'd never had it like that alive." She gave me my change and did not smile, and I did not either, and it was as good a thing as anyone has said to me this month. I stood with the flowers against my chest and could not think where to put my eyes.

At the far end, where the tools are, a man with a tray on trestles had a row of rusted things laid out on newspaper: planes, a brace, a set of feeler gauges, a few keys on a loop of wire. And a bicycle pump. Brass, with a wooden handle gone dark from hands, and a short rubber hose with a bayonet connector, the sort my father had on the rigger's bench. Two pounds. He said, "Works. I'll show you," and pushed the plunger down against his palm and it hissed and pushed back. I bought it before he'd finished. The flowers went under my arm and the pump went in my jacket pocket, where it hung heavy and slapped my hip.

By the tea van outside the east door there was a bicycle leaning on the railings, a lady's one with a wicker basket and a bell, and the back tyre soft to the rim, the whole wheel sat down on it like an old woman on a step. No one near it. I stood a moment. I thought, it isn't mine. Then I crouched, which I do more carefully these days, and unscrewed the little black cap, and the connector went on with a click, and I pumped. It took forty strokes. My arm knew how. The tyre came up round and hard under my thumb, and the pump kept its own small breath on every stroke. A woman in a green cardigan came out of the fruit stall with a bag of clementines, looked at me, looked at the bike, and said, "That's mine." I said the tyre had been down. "It's always down," she said. "I leave it and it's down. I thought it was the valve." I said it was probably the valve. She said, "How much do I owe you?" and I said nothing, and she said, "Well," and put the clementines in the basket and pedalled away, wobbling, the bell going once by accident. I stayed there with the pump in my hand and a smell of hot rubber on my fingers. I was pleased. Two pounds, and it did what it said.

Then, because I was restless, and because I had the flowers and there was no hurry to go back, I went round by the secondhand books. A man with a folding table and a cash tin, and boxes of paperbacks with the spines cracked white. Somebody's whole shelf of Reader's Digest Condensed. Somebody's gardening. I turned a box with my foot. There was a thin one with a leather glove on the cover, a bird's feet gripping it, and inside the front page a name in pencil and the price rubbed out. I read where it fell open. The bird is flown at a weight, and the weight is the control, because a bird that is not hungry does not return. Somebody had underlined not hungry and put a small tick in the margin. I read it twice, standing there. The man said, "Fifty pence to you, that one," and I said I was only looking, and put it back on the top of the box the wrong way up, and turned it round again.

It was one when I got back to the yard. I sat on the landing with the sausage roll and the blue thermos and the pump beside me on the stair like a dog. The tea came out hot through the whole of that cold stairwell, and the strip light did its flicker. I had left the fire door open and there was sun in the yard, a long white bar of it on the concrete, and I watched dust go through it. Kieran came out with the chamois and said, "That's a nice pump." I said it was a good pump. He said his own bike was in the shed and the back was going, and I said bring it, and heard myself say it. He said, "Would you?" and I said I would, if he had a spanner, and then I stopped, because I do not know what I was about to say next.

The afternoon was the laying out of a woman from Peverell. Small, ninety-one, the hands folded already. The window in the prep room was open a hand's width and the warm came in on the back of my neck, and the smell of the road with it, tar softening. I did her hair. It was very fine. I did the nails, which somebody had painted pink not long before, one thumb chipped. I looked for her shoes on the chair. Slippers. I stood there with the comb and thought of the towpath and the hail and the compost lying black in it, and how it is warm now and you'd never think.

I did not take the bucket back. I keep meaning to. It is in the boot of the Octavia with a coat of earth dried on it, and the old man with the white beard said it was the only one.

Home by six. Light still in the sky. I put both windows up at the back and the fire off, and the house went on ticking anyway, from habit. Eggs. I sat down for them this time. Bosun took his blanket to the back door and lay across the step with his nose over the sill, and Wafer went out through the flap without a look at anyone. Pilsudski watched her go from the third stair and did not move.

At seven I took the washing in and saw Barry. He was out in his shirtsleeves in his own garden, a can in one hand, standing with his weight on one leg, looking at his hedge as if it had said something. I had the sheet in my arms. "Evening," he said. "Lovely." I said it was. Behind him the shed had its side to the sun, and against the back wall, under the guttering, where I had seen them on Sunday, stood the loppers, the red handles gone dull, the blades out and a fur of rust along the edge. He saw where I was looking. He looked at the shed, and then at me, and I saw him decide something. "That hedge," he said, "is going to be the death of me." I said nothing. I put the sheet over my shoulder and the corner of it dragged on the path.

"Anything you want doing on your side," he said, "just say."

I said thanks. I went in. I stood in the kitchen with the sheet and the smell of it, sun and dust, and I heard the can go down on his wall, and I thought, he has not said.
