Twenty to six, and the cold had an edge on it you could have cut bread with. The window was clear again. The frost had drawn its white line along the fence tops, and beyond it the hedge stood black and separate against a sky going the colour of the inside of a shell. I lay and looked. Bosun was across my shins, breathing through his nose. I turned my head on the pillow, left ear down, and heard the pipes start to knock, one, two, a gap, one. I turned back. Nothing was asked of me for a minute or two, and I took it.

The kitchen was cold enough to see my breath by the cooker. The cupboard door was still in the hall against the wall, next to the letter, so the cupboard stood open on its tins like a mouth left open in sleep. I looked at the beans and the three pink fish and Mum's pears. The compost was still where I'd swept it, a low black ridge along the skirting that I'd missed, and the cup I'd kept back was on the windowsill with a teaspoon standing up in it, for no reason. I put the kettle on. The tea went into the Argyle mug with the chipped handle, and I drank the first of it standing, with my thumb on the chip.

At seven the drill started next door. Three short, one long. I'd been ready for it and it still went through me, in the plaster, in the rack of cups. I stood with the mug and counted the gaps. It's a habit I've got from ringing Nula. Eleven seconds, nine, fourteen. Then a man's voice, flat, to nobody, and the long one again. I thought, what does a man want with that much hole at seven on a Saturday. Then I thought that I had a hole of my own the size of my little finger, and that I couldn't stand there being superior with it in my hand.

Reg rang at ten past. Not his daughter this time, Reg himself, in the voice he keeps for the telephone, loud and a bit formal, as if the line went all the way to Truro. "Piotr. It's Reg. Up at the plots." I said I knew. He said he'd been up at five and put his foot down out of the bed and it had told him no. "Knee's the size of a swede," he said. "Doctor says rest it. I says, rest it where. So I'm sorry about the barrow, and the manure'll keep, it's not going anywhere, it's in a heap." I said I'd go up on my own and start the trench. He said, "Don't. You'll do it wrong." I said how would I do it wrong. "You'll do it too neat," he said. "Wait for me. Next Saturday." And then, before I'd answered, "Is that a drill I can hear?" I said it was next door. "Tell him it's a bad time of year for it," Reg said, as if that meant anything, and rang off.

So it was twice. Yesterday's plan kicked to today, and today's kicked to the week after, and I'd stood there each time with the phone against my chest like something handed back. I don't mind. I said to the cats that I didn't mind. Wafer looked at me from the top of the fridge, and Pilsudski, on the third stair, shut his eyes.

The washing machine man rang at half past eight, the young hoarse one. He said he was sorry, but it was going to have to be the Tuesday after all, not the Monday, because the Brixham job had gone on and the part was on a van somewhere between Exeter and here. Eight to twelve. I said I'd got Denny lined up for the Monday, or nearly. He said, "Ah," in a way that told me he'd been fond of the Monday too. I said Tuesday would be fine. I wasn't sure Denny could do Tuesday. I wasn't sure Denny could do Monday, either, since I hadn't asked. I wrote it on the calendar above the pig, under the crossed-out second, in the little space that was left: WASHER, TUES, 8-12. Then I looked at the crossed-out one and the thirtieth of April beside it, and thought, that's a lot of writing on one piece of paper for a man who says he isn't busy.

I did the compost then. I'd been leaving it, the way you leave a thing till it's the only job left. Wet on the lino it had dried to a crust with the cat tracks pressed in it, and it came up in flakes, and the fine stuff went under the cooker with the beer mat. I got down and lay on my good side with my cheek nearly on the floor, and the light came in low under the units and showed me everything under there: the beer mat, two pennies, a bottle top, a dog biscuit gone grey. My arm went in to the shoulder. It was very cold under the cooker and smelled of an old kitchen, chip fat and dust. I brought the lot out on the shovel and looked at it, and it was more than I'd thought. I put the beer mat in the bin. Then I took it out again, because it was a good mat, Courage, with a ship on it, and I stood it on the windowsill against the cup of compost. That's a pair of things I'd not have chosen to keep.

The cup I did something with in the end. I took the window box in from the back step, and it was frozen to the shape of last year, a grey lump with a dead geranium in it like a hand. I broke the top up with the spoon. It took a while. Then I scooped the good black compost from the cup and spread it, a thin dark skin over the frost, and pressed it down with my thumb. Nothing was going in it. It was far too early for anything. But it looked like a thing that someone meant, and I stood at the sink and washed my hands under the cold tap till they hurt, and the drill went again next door, and the water shook in the plughole.

At eleven I went round.

I took the door with me, since it was what I had. It was light enough under one arm, chipboard and a bit of white laminate with the hinge plate still screwed on, hanging by the one good hole. I went out the front and along the pavement, past the hedge end, and up to number 14, and I didn't rehearse. The van was in the drive with its back doors open and a stepladder in it. I could hear a radio, a man singing something with a lot of trumpets. Barry came round the side of the house in a sleeveless fleece with sawdust in the hair on his arms and the drill still in his fist, pointed at the ground like a man who's forgotten he's holding it.

"Piotr," he said. He said it right, near enough, Pyotr, which I've never known him do.

I held up the door. I don't know what I thought it would say. He looked at it for a long time, and at the crumbled hole, and then at me, and something went across his face that wasn't what I'd been ready for.

"Chipboard," he said. "They all go. Forty years, that stuff, and then it's cake." He put the drill down on the bonnet of the van. "You want a dowel and some glue. I've got some of both in the shed, hang on."

He was gone some time. I stood on his drive with the door in my arms and the sun on the back of my neck and the frost dripping off the guttering in a slow row, one, two, one, and I looked at what he'd been drilling. It was the side gate. He'd taken the old bolt off and was fitting a new one, a proper black one with a hasp, and the holes were neat and clean and there was a little pile of pale dust below each. Not the fence. I thought I'd known that. I thought, I didn't know that.

He came back with three lengths of dowel in a paper bag and a tube of the yellow glue, the good kind, half used. "Push it in the hole, wet, and snap it off flush and leave it overnight. Don't put the screw in till tomorrow, it won't hold." He pushed it at me. "Keep the tube. I've another." I said I'd bring it back. "Don't," said Barry, and picked up the drill.

I was at the gate before I thought that I'd meant to say something. About the hedge. Or the letter, which I hadn't mentioned and neither had he, though it had been on the doorstep since August and both of us knew it. I turned round. He'd gone back to the bolt and had his ear near it, listening to it slide, one thumb on the hasp, the way you listen to a lock. Then the drill went again, and I thought, well, it can be another day.

I did the hinge at the kitchen table on newspaper, with the door upside down and the light coming in across it. It smelled of yellow glue and bread, oddly, and of the frost still on my sleeve.
