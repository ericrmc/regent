# 2019-03-22

Twenty to six, and no rain on the roof for the first time in a week. I lay and listened to the quiet, which was full of small drips: the gutter at the back, the spitting length, running down to a last few beads. Bosun was on my shins. Wafer had come back in the night and was in the crook of my knee, small and hot as a loaf. Pilsudski was on the third stair. I carried the dog.

Tea, the thumb on the chip. Toast. The jam was nearly at the bottom of the jar, and I ran the knife round the shoulder of the glass to get the last of it, and thought I would need to go in to the deli on Monday and not make a thing of it.

At ten past seven there was a man on the step in a yellow vest with a box under his arm and a scanner in his hand. He was about twenty-five, with a wet-looking fringe and a face that had already been told off once that morning. He said, "Fourteen?" and I said fourteen was the next door, the one with the hedge, and he looked at the box and said no, it said Outland Avenue. I said this was Outland Road. He said, "They're the same, aren't they?" I said they were not, and that the Avenue was the other side of the park, behind the school, and he said the satnav had brought him here. The box said FRAGILE on three sides and had a woman's name on it, Ash, and the sound it made when he tilted it was of something loose and glassy. I had a hand out before I knew it. I said I could take it in and he could sort it out, and he said he wasn't allowed to leave it with a different address, and then he said, "You're all right, mate," as if I had offered him a lift. He went down the path holding the box out in front of him like a man with a bowl of soup.

I stood there a minute longer than I needed. From the step you see the top of Barry's fence over the side passage, the new grey panels and the old ones behind them, and the leylandii looking over the lot. I had my keys in my hand and I did not go and look at it properly.

The removal was at Derriford, a woman of ninety-one from Ward 12, and Terry drove. He parked on Level 6 because the ground floor was full, and the machine on the way in ate two of my pound coins before it took the third. The car park on a weekday morning is a quiet sort of frantic. People walk fast with their faces set. A woman was sitting in a Fiesta with both hands on the wheel and the engine off, and she didn't move the whole time we were there. Gulls on the barrier, fat, unbothered. An ambulance went down the ramp with its lights but no siren.

We were coming back up with the trolley folded in the van when the hail came, out of a sky that had been ordinary a minute before. It hit the roof of the van like a handful of gravel thrown by somebody who meant it, and the concrete went white, and the noise on Level 6, with no walls to speak of, was enormous. It bounced off the bonnets. Terry said something and I couldn't hear it. I sat with my hands on my knees and watched a piece the size of a pea roll along the dash and melt, and then it was over, ninety seconds at the most, the stones lying in the corners of the bays as if they'd been tipped from a bag, and the sun on them. A man walked out from behind a pillar and stood looking at his windscreen. Terry said again, "I said that's a bit much," and I said it was.

I rang the vet from the top of the ramp, where there is one bar in the corner by the stairwell door. It rang and rang and then a recorded girl said they were sorry to have missed me and to press one for reception, and I pressed nothing and let it go. Monday, half past ten. I'd think of something. Marta will say take the hour and I will take the hour.

I did not go back to the yard. It was a quarter to one and my hair was down on my collar in the way that Nula would have mentioned in the corridor without turning her head. Mutley Plain, past the deli, and down the side to Sal's, which has a striped pole that hasn't turned since I was thirty and a row of four chairs, three of them occupied. I sat by the window with the paper. The place smelled of talc and hot towels and the blue stuff in the jar of combs. Sal had a sports jacket on over the barber's gown and did not ask what I wanted. He never has. He put the cloth round my neck and did the thing with two fingers at the nape, the cold clipper, and the sound of it just behind my ears took the day off me a bit at a time.

In the next chair there was a man of sixty or so with a broken nose and hands like shovels, in overalls with sawdust in the creases. He was talking across Sal's other customer to a lad in the third chair, who was maybe sixteen and had been brought in by his father and looked it. He said, "You bore the hole through the tenon a bit short of the one in the cheek. Just off. Then when you drive the pin it has to bend to get through, and it drags the joint up tight as it goes in. So the fastening and the clamping's the one action. No cramps. It just pulls itself shut." The lad said, "What if it splits?" "Then you've drove it too hard," said the man, "or your oak's no good." Sal said something about the football. The man said, "Two hundred years, some of them." Then his own barber, a boy with a ponytail, took the cloth off him with a snap and he stood and felt the back of his neck.

I paid Sal eleven pounds and gave him one for himself. Outside, the air on my neck was the shock of it, the newly bare place, cool and slightly stinging. I put my hand up and it was a stranger's head, the bristle at the back like a doormat. I walked back the long way, up the side of the church, and the gutters were still running with hail-water and the little stones had collected in the drain grates like frogspawn.

The afternoon I don't remember well. Cloths, brush, wheel arches. Marta went past twice and on the second time said, "You've had your hair cut," and I said yes, and she said it suited me, and I said it would grow. Dennis Pym did not ring. I noticed that I had been listening for it.

Home at half past five. The house was quiet in the way it is when three animals are waiting for you and pretending they are not. I did the round: Bosun's dish, the cats' two dishes, Wafer's water. I hung up my jacket. In the hall there's the shelf by the door with the pig on it, a fat china one with a slot in its back that Roisin gave me for coins, which now weighs down what it weighs down. The brown envelope was where I'd left it on Tuesday night, one corner sticking out from under the pig's belly. Barry's writing on the front, my name and Number 12 in blue biro, pressed hard. I did not pick it up. I have read it once, and I know the number in it, eighteen inches, and I know the name Mr Trewin, which is written the way someone writes a name they have practised. I stood looking at the flap. I wondered whether a man who writes to you from the other side of a fence has to lick the gum himself, and what that tastes like. It seemed a reasonable thing to wonder. I put my hand on the pig's cold back, and I moved it maybe a quarter of an inch, and put it back where it had been.

I ate standing at the counter. Six pierogi, from the freezer, the potato and cheese, with fried onion, out of the pan and on to the plate and then not even on to the plate for the last two. There is something about a hot dumpling that you can eat with the fingers if nobody is watching. Mama would have hit me with the tea towel. Bosun sat by my foot with his chin lifted at the exact height for a dropped one, and I dropped one. Wafer looked on from the top of the fridge.

Afterwards I took the washing out. The two shirts and the towel from yesterday's trial had sat in the drum all night and smelled faintly of damp, so I put them through again, and stood and watched the round window with the tea in my hand, and at the spin there was no thump at all. I found that I was smiling at a machine.

The back door showed me the garden going blue. There was still white in the corners of the lawn, a scatter of hail that hadn't melted where the shadow lay, and I went out in my slippers and picked up a stone and held it, and it was only wet. On the far side the fence stood at its angle, the new panels a little proud. I paced along it, heel to toe, on my own side, and stopped at the corner post, and stood there with my hands in my pockets looking at where the grass changed, or where I thought it did.
