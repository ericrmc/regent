Twenty to six and the window over the sink was already open, because I had left it open, because last night was the first night in months I couldn't smell the cold in the house. The air that came in had a green smell, cut grass from somewhere, and a warmth in it before the sun was properly up. Bosun was flat on the lino in the one patch of light with his legs out behind him like a frog. Pilsudski was on the third stair with his eyes shut and pretended he wasn't watching. Tea, thumb on the chip.

Toast. I got the jar down from the shelf and it was the end of it. I ran the knife round the sides and got a spoonful and a bit, thin, more smear than jam, and put it on the one slice and ate the other slice with margarine and stood there chewing. That was when I remembered the other jar. The one she'd tied with the pastry string. I had it in my hand at the counter, and then the business of the change, and the tin for the shelter, and then I was out on the pavement with the blue card. I have no memory of it in my hand after that. Not in the coat, not on the hook at work. It is sitting on her counter, then, wrapped up like a christening present, and she'll have put it under the till and thought, well, he doesn't say much. I stood at the sink and was more annoyed than a jar deserves. I'd have said it wasn't like me, but it is exactly like me. It's what I do now, I go and get the thing and leave it.

The machine. I had decided the night before. If you can't fix a thing, look at it properly and say so, that's what my father did with a shackle, held it up to the light and dropped it in the bin bucket without a word. So I pulled the rubber seal back from the door and ran a finger round the fold. It was black in there, soft, and it had gone right through in two places, not a hole but a split you could have put a pencil in. The gaffer tape was in my coat, in the hall, and I fetched it, and I stood there with the roll in my hand. I said yesterday I wouldn't. I did it anyway. Two strips, pressed in, the silver looking ridiculous against the white rubber. Then I put a hot wash on with nothing in it and sat on the kitchen chair and watched. It took four minutes. Not a weep this time, a tongue of brown water came out over the bottom edge of the door and across the lino and reached the beer mat, which I had not moved, and picked it up and turned it over. I turned the machine off at the wall. Bosun got up and walked round the puddle with great care and went and sat in the hall. I took the tape off, which took some time, and it left a grey gum along the seal, and I did not clean it. I got the mop. It is a launderette machine now, the Plain, the pound coins pushed flat with a thumb. I wrote nothing down. There is a sort of relief in it, that I hadn't expected, and I didn't trust that either.

The little funeral was at eleven at Efford, a man of eighty-eight called Pascoe, seven in the chapel and one of them in a wheelchair, and the sun on the crematorium lawn so bright the daffodils looked too yellow to be true. Marta conducted. I carried with Kieran at the front and a lad from the firm on hire at the back, who I didn't know, who was sweating in his collar. The weight was nothing. The wheelchair man, the son, I think, had a hat on his knee and rubbed the brim round and round with his thumb, and when we came out past him he looked at the coffin and said, quite clearly, "Well, Dad," and no more. Nobody said anything. There was a blackbird going on and on in the shrubs by the door as if it hadn't been told.

I had filled the thermos at six. It is a green Thermos with a dent in the base, my father's or my mother's, I can never think which, and it has kept tea hot enough to scald since before I was at Voisey's. In the hearse yard at half past twelve I unscrewed the cup and poured, and steam did not come. It was tea the way a bath is tea. I put my hand round the flask and it was cool as a bottle of milk. I have done this three times this week, and I keep thinking it's the cork, the stopper, and I looked at it, and it's not, it's inside, whatever they had in there, the glass or the vacuum, gone. I shook it by my ear. There is a little rattle in it, a sound like a beetle in a matchbox. I drank the tea anyway, standing by the bins with the sun on my back, because I had made it. Kieran came over with a cup from the kettle inside, brown as a boot, and I said no, and he said, "Go on," and I said no again, more sharply than I'd meant. He said, "Right you are," and went off and drank it himself. I could hear him being careful with the door.

I had the sausage roll on the back stairs, on the middle landing, the light flickering a bit, and did not look at the bit of wall where I always look.

The car park at Derriford was full by two and I went round twice before I found a space on Level 6, and stood at the machine with my coins ready, having memorised, for a change, which slot. Three pounds forty. It gave me a ticket. The lift smelled of hand gel. The desk at physio had a girl with a lanyard and a plastic tray of pens on chains, and I put the letter down in front of her and the phone beside it, the text, open. I had my speech. She looked at the screen, and at her own screen, and said, "Oh, you didn't reply YES, so it hasn't moved you. You're still down for today." I said the text said the Thursday. She said, "It does, but you have to confirm. If you don't, it stays." I said, "Then why send it." She said, "It's automatic," and gave me a clipboard. I had a good deal more to say and there was nobody to say it to, again, so I took the clipboard.

It was a new form, for the new clinic, two pages, with a drawing of a body from the front and the back, and you were to shade where it hurt. I sat in a chair that was bolted to the next chair. I filled in my name, Mahon, and put the date, and shaded the back of the neck, and beside it, in the little box, ticked a side. I did not read it. It was quarter past two and the sun coming through the glass wall was on my hands and I was thinking about the tea. Gemma came and called my name the way they do, all the vowels in it, and took me through, and read the form as I lay down on the couch, and said, "It says here right?" I said what. She said, "You've ticked right side. But it's the left that's stiff, when you turn?" I said it was the left. She said not to worry, she'd cross it out and initial it. And did, with a small neat line, and wrote G.T. above it, and that was that, but it sits there now in my head, the little line, and the word RIGHT under it, in my own capitals, as though the body had told the truth and I'd been the one who changed it.

She pushed her thumbs into the muscle beside the bone, and it went off like a light. I said something in Polish, one of my mother's, and Gemma said, "Sorry?" and I said it was nothing, and she said, "It sounded like something." I said it was for the neck.

Afterwards I did not sit in the car. It was twenty past three, and the day had got hot. On Level 6 the concrete gave the heat back at me from the floor, and the gulls on the barrier had their beaks open. I drove, and I did not know I was going to, out along the Plym to the bridge and left where the road stops, and left the Octavia at the end by the two white gates, and walked in. They call it the towpath. I have never known what was towed on it. It is the old track through the woods above the river, flat, cinders and mud, with the water somewhere down through the trees making a noise like a sheet being shaken. The trees are barely in leaf. It was the light, all that pale green, coming through and lying down on the path in pieces. I had my jacket over my arm and took my tie off, and put it in my pocket, and undid a button, and my neck felt as if someone had lifted a hand off it.

Two men were coming the other way with a spaniel between them and a lot of talk. The taller one was doing the talking. "No, sixty-seven, sixty-eight. That's your body. You go down to sixty-three, it's thin, it's dry, it'll ferment out to nothing, you'll be drinking water with a hat on. But hold it up and you leave the sugars in and it's got a weight to it, you see. It's all the one number. You don't touch anything else, you just hold your nerve on the one number for an hour." The other said his wife wouldn't have the pan in the house. "You do it in the shed," said the tall man, and they went past me, and the dog gave me a look, and the words went on behind them up the path for some time, one number, one number, and then the water covered them.

There was a bench where the path bends by the old bridge, and on the arm of it a foil balloon had been tied, silver, a big number nine, a bit deflated, nodding. Somebody's party, or somebody had left it. I sat down on the other end and got out the flask and looked at it. Then I looked at the nine.

Sunday. It was Sunday, the thirty-first. It was Roisin's birthday. She is fifty-two, no, fifty-one, it doesn't matter. I was standing at the folding table in the launderette thinking about the duvet and the machine at Stoke, and it was that day, I was thinking about her without knowing what for, and the phone went with the hospital and I never once thought of the date. Two days. I had a whole trick, for years, of putting it on the calendar in the hall, a small cross in pencil, and after we split I went on doing it for four years, and then some year I didn't, and I did not notice not doing it. I sat there with the cold tea. I said, aloud, "Well." The balloon turned round on its string.

I rang. I do not know that I meant to until the phone was at my ear. It rang five times, six, and then there was her voice, with a noise round it, a clatter, a radio, and she said, "Piotr?" the way she does, like a door opening a crack. I said I was ringing about Sunday, that I'd, and she said, "Hang on, hang on, I'll go outside," and I heard the door, and I heard a man's voice say something, low, not a word, just the shape of a question, and she said "It's only" and then the trees took it. The bar went to nothing. The screen said Call Failed and the little picture of the path.
