# 2019-03-28

Ten past three by the clock on the landing, which I know because I got up and looked, and by the time I had got back into bed it was twenty to four, the way it goes. Rain. Not the soft stuff of the last two nights. This came sideways against the back bedroom and drummed on the flat bit of roof over the kitchen like a man with a grievance. Bosun had gone under the bed, where he goes for thunder, and there was no thunder. I lay with my eyes open and went through the week the way you go through pockets. The vet on Monday and the Trewin at ten. The loppers. The Derriford card on the chair under my jacket, which I could feel from the bed as if it were warm.

At half past four I gave up and went down. Pilsudski was on the third stair with his ears flat, awake as well, and I said, "You too," and he looked through me at the front door. Bosun came down behind me pressed to my calf and stopped dead at the cat. I lifted him over. He weighs what a bag of potatoes weighs, and he shivers like one that's been left in a car.

Tea, thumb on the chip. Toast with nothing on, because the jar is a smear now and I'm not a man who will go at a smear with a knife twice. The window over the sink was running so hard I couldn't see Barry's fence. The garden was a grey sheet with the path going under it. I stood there and drank the tea and thought about nothing, and then about the compost on the beans bed, all three heaps, and where it was going.

I walked in. I ought to have taken the car but I didn't feel like sitting in it. I had the big black umbrella from the hall, the one we keep for the family at the graveside, and it took the wind like a sail. By Peverell the gutters were running like a weir and the whole road was empty. No one at the bus stop, no one with a dog, a bus going by with its lights on and the lower deck vacant, the driver looking straight ahead as if it were his own fault. A single carrier bag went past me down the middle of the road at about my speed. On the Plain the deli had its shutter half up and the light on inside, and I could see the woman at the back with her hands in a bowl, not looking out. I did not slow down. Water went into my left shoe at the crossing and stayed there.

The yard at Voisey's was a lake. The gulley by the garage door had a fan of plane leaves across it and the water stood ankle deep and turned slowly on itself. I went in with my trouser legs stuck to my shins and stood on the mat in the back passage dripping like something brought in. Marta's coat was on the peg, so she was in already, and I could hear the kettle upstairs.

I rang the vet at nine from the middle landing, since I'd told myself I would. Under the light, back against the wall, the cream paint cold through my shirt and the whole stairwell smelling of wet wool and drains, the way it does when the rain gets in. I had Monday written on my hand again, nearly gone from the wet. It rang six times and a machine picked up, a woman's voice, calm as a tea towel: "You're through to the surgery. Please hold, your call is important to us." Music. I held. Something with a flute. I stood with the receiver against my good ear and watched the light on the landing flicker its little flicker, and heard Alan's feet cross the office floor over my head, and a door, and then the line went. Not a click. Just the flute, and then a hum, and then nothing at all. I stood with the dead receiver. I did not ring back. I put it on the cradle and thought I would do it after the ten o'clock, and I know what I mean when I think that.

It was then that Marta came down with the clipboard. "Sorry," she said. "Alan wants everyone's done by Friday. It's for the insurance, the staff ones. Name, address, doctor, next of kin, who to ring." I said I'd been here twenty-three years, he'd got it all. "He hasn't got it on his new sheet," she said, and held it out, and the biro swung on its bit of string against the rail. "Sit down if you like. Don't stand there dripping on it."

So I sat on the third stair with the clipboard on my knee, on the green rubber tread, wet through. Voisey & Daughter along the top, and under it a line in small type: for the purposes of the firm's records only. My name. The address, the postcode, which I know as I know my own teeth. Date of birth. Doctor, and I had to think, because I go to the same one as I've always gone to and could not have told you his name. Then Next of Kin. I wrote Roisin Keogh. I wrote the Stoke address. Then a box, Relationship, and I sat with the biro over it. Partner was true for eleven years. The pen made a small blot. I looked at the blot, and then I went on to the box below, Any Medical Conditions We Should Know Of, and wrote none, which is not quite so either, and gave it back to Marta with Relationship blank. She looked at it. She looked at me. "I'll leave it for you," she said, and took the clipboard away up the stairs without another word, and the biro on its string knocked all the way up the rail like a tongue against a tooth.

At half past nine the rain came down properly. I don't know what it had been doing before. You couldn't see the yard wall from the prep room. There was a moment in the office when Marta and I stood at the window and neither of us said anything, and it went on for about two minutes, all that water, and the gulley gave up and the yard became one grey pane with the Mercedes standing in it up to their hubcaps. "That's the ten o'clock cancelled for the flowers, then," she said. "They'll never get here." The florist had rung to say the van wouldn't leave Crownhill. The family were coming anyway.

They were eleven of them under a single big golfing umbrella at the crematorium, the rest not coming, and the man's sister held the umbrella and would not let it go even into the porch, so that the water ran off the ribs of it down my collar while I stood with the door. Dennis was not there. Kieran and I and a man from the family took him, and two of the men from the chapel staff, and it was a light coffin for a heavy day. The hymn was almost lost under the roof. I could hear it in the gaps, the way you hear a radio in another room. I watched the sister's shoes, black patent, filling up with water, and she did not look down once.

Back at the yard the bottom step of the stairwell was under an inch. I stood in the passage taking my socks off, which nobody sees. Kieran came in behind me shaking himself like a dog. "That's the wettest I've ever been," he said, "and I fell in the Cattewater once." He was pleased with it. He hung his jacket on the radiator and stood there steaming, twenty-four and quite content, and said his bike was still in the shed and he'd not bring it in this, and I said no, not in this. He looked at the middle landing where the handrail bracket has been hanging off since Christmas, swinging when you lean on it, and said, "That's coming off, that." I said I knew. I said I'd been meaning to. It's a fair fault to find in a man who goes up and down it twice a day.

So after the sausage roll, which I ate standing because the landing was wet where the wall sweats, I went to look for screws. There is a tin in the cupboard under the bottom flight, where the boiler is and the spare bulbs and the tin of black touch-up for the hearse. A Peek Freans tin with the lid dented in one corner and a picture of a Highland cow going brown. It was Nula's. I do not know whose it was before that. It is heavy when you lift it and it does not rattle so much as slide, a long shifting noise like shingle when you turn it. I took it to the landing and knelt with it on the tread. Brass slotted, some blued, a great many of the black japanned ones nobody uses now. A few screw-eyes. A cup hook. Three little chrome caps for something. A twist of newspaper with something rattling in it that had once been labelled in her pencil and was now just a smear of grey. And under it, when I went down with my fingers, a cabin hook and a brass shoehorn, of all things, the size of a finger, with a hole at the end.

Kieran held the torch. I said he needn't and he said he'd rather. I tried four for length and none of them had a thread I liked. The bracket's old hole in the plaster was wide as a two-pence piece. I put my thumb over it. There was a smell off the wall of damp lime and old paint. "Do you keep them in any order?" Kieran said, looking into the tin. I said no. "There's an order somewhere," he said, "there's got to be," and I said if there had been I would have put my hand on it. I found one at last, long, brass, a bit dull, that bit when I started it. I turned it in until the bracket stood up firm on its own. I put my full weight on the rail. It took it. Kieran tried it too, and swung his whole body on it like a child on a gate. "Yeah," he said. "Yeah, that's good." I put the tin back where it lives. I stood a moment with the cupboard door in my hand. Something in me wanted the noise of it again and I didn't lift it.

The afternoon was a laying out, a woman from Mannamead, eighty-three, a good face, the rain still loud on the prep room skylight. I had the little radio on the shelf on low, Nula's, the one with the tape across the dial. A woman was talking about rivers, in the calm voice they have. I was drying my hands. "…so what you're measuring is the lag," she said, "between the rainfall and the peak flow, and that tells you about the ground and not the rain." Then something else about a bridge in Somerset, and a man laughing, and then a piece of choir music with no words. I dried between my fingers. I did the hair, which was long, and had been put up by somebody else for the last fortnight, and I let it down and brushed it forward, and looked at the shoes on the chair, brown, with a strap, worn at the outer edge of the heel. I thought, she walked on the outside of her feet. Then I went out into the passage for the towels, and the gulley in the yard was still running full, though the sky had gone pale over the wall and you could see the rain was thinning.

I left at five. It had stopped, or something near enough. The street was wet and shining and empty still, a street after a party, and somebody's bin had blown over on Outland Road and lay in the gutter with its lid off. There was a gap in the cloud above the Hoe with the light coming through it yellow. I thought I might go up to the plot and see what had become of the compost. I did not. I went home.

The house was cold and smelled of dog. Two of the pierogi from the freezer, ruskie, and I fried them in butter until they had a skin, and sat and ate them with a fork and a bit of sour cream out of the tub. Bosun came out from wherever he'd been all day and lay on my foot. Wafer sat on the sill and looked out at the rain that had stopped, as if it might be persuaded to start up again.

At seven I went to the back door to look at the light, and stood on the step. The fence was black with wet. Barry's shed had its door pulled to and the padlock through the hasp, and the guttering above it was still pouring in a thin rope onto the concrete. I looked at the wall under it. There was nothing leaning there. No red handles, no blades. The place where they'd been was a dry oblong on the concrete and everything round it wet. So he'd taken them in, or somebody had.
