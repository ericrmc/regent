Twenty to six, and the first thing was the light. The curtain has a gap at the edge where it doesn't meet the rail, and a line of white came through it and lay across the blanket and Bosun's back. Cold, clear, a frost on the inside of the window glass in the corner. I lay and looked at it. Wafer was on the pillow next to my ear, purring in her sleep like a small engine turning over. The rain has gone and left all that sharpness behind it, as if somebody had wiped the day.

Pilsudski on the third stair. Bosun in my arms, warm and stiff and heavy, his eyes on the cat over my shoulder the whole way down. Nobody spoke.

Tea, the thumb on the chip. Toast with what was left of the jam. I scraped the glass with the knife and got a smear the size of a fifty pence piece, and I ate it standing at the back door with the door open a hand's width to let the cold in, and the garden was grey and silver, every blade of the lawn with its own white edge.

The bag of compost was where I'd left it on Thursday, on the kitchen floor between the cooker and the cupboard I hung. I had meant it for the allotment this morning, for the bean trench, and I bent to lift it and got my arms under the bottom of it, and the seam went. Not a tear, a long unpicking down the side, and all the dark wet weight of it came out across the lino in one slide, over my slippers and into the dog's water dish. It smelled sweet and rotten at once, the smell of a wet forest floor. I stood with half a bag in my arms and the rest round my feet, and I did not say anything, and I noticed that I was not angry. I put the bag down. I got the dustpan and the coal shovel from the porch and began, and it took a good while, because compost on lino is not like other dirt, it stays damp and gets into the pattern. I went down on my knees with my neck pulling and scooped it into a bucket, handful by handful. There was a slug in it. I put the slug out on the step.

The cupboard door behind me hung open, as it does, a quarter inch off the frame, and I looked at it from the floor. From down there you see it truest. The bottom corner stands proud of the carcass and no amount of turning the little screws has ever brought it in. Barry's level said so, or I said so, or the counter did. I shut it with my knee and it swung out again slowly, like a tired person leaving.

At ten to seven the noise started next door. A drill first, a proper hammer drill, and then a whine that rose and fell, and I knew before I looked that he'd got the saw out. On a Saturday, at ten to seven. It came through the party wall and the kitchen window, and Bosun got up from the hall carpet and came and stood against my calf, and Wafer went over the top of the fridge and along the shelf and out of sight. I listened to it. I thought that a man who writes to you about eighteen inches must have to cut it somewhere. It stopped, and there was a clatter of something dropped, a long metallic rattle, and Barry said a word to himself that I heard as clearly as if he had been at my elbow. Then the drill again. I finished the floor with the wet cloth and rinsed it at the sink, and the water ran brown and then clear.

At half past seven the phone went and it was Roisin. She said, "Are you up? I knew you'd be up." I said I was, and that I was on the floor with a bag of compost. She said, "Listen, don't come today. About the gutters. I've got it muddled. My sister's coming over with the kids and it'll be a madhouse and I don't want you up a ladder in front of the whole family." I said it was no trouble. I said the ladder was already on the roof of the car, which was a small lie, it was in the yard. She said, "Next week, or the week after. It's not going anywhere." There was a sound behind her, a radio or a kettle, and a moment when I thought she was going to say something else, she took the breath, and then she said, "Right, then," and I said, "Right," and she rang off. I stood with the phone against my chest. The saw started again next door. I had the whole morning now with nowhere to put it.

I made a second tea. Then I took the split bag, and the half of it that was left, and folded the top over and stood it up in a bucket, which is a way of keeping it together, and put the bucket in the boot of the Octavia along with the fork and the hessian sack and the sprouted seed potatoes in their egg boxes, the ones I've had on the window sill of the back bedroom since February, with their pale stubby shoots going purple at the tips.

The phone again as I was locking the door. A girl's voice, the one from the vet, quick and a little breathless, apologising before she had said who she was. "Mr Mahon? It's Peverell, it's about Bosun. I'm so sorry, I did try you Thursday, I think we got cut off." I said we had. She said the vet would be out on Monday morning after all, a family thing, and could they move him to Wednesday, ten past four. I said I'd have to see about Wednesday. I said I had thought Monday was going to be the difficult one and now it wasn't, and she laughed politely without knowing why. "Ten past four," she said, "I'll put you down for it, and if you can't, ring us." I said I would ring. I did not write it on anything. I said it twice to myself going down the path, ten past four Wednesday, ten past four, and I could feel it slipping already like something held loosely in a wet hand.

The Ridgeway site at nine. The road up there was bright and stony with the frost still lying in the ruts where the sun didn't reach, and the gate, when I lifted it, let go with its usual squeal, and the whole of the plots lay out in front of me, flat and glittering, the netting stiff, every cane with a white cap. Pigeons on the roof of somebody's shed. A radio somewhere, low, playing a song I nearly knew. Nobody about but Reg, of course, at the tap on the end of the row in his flat cap and his long brown coat, filling a can with a great deal of ceremony and looking at me over the top of it.

"You're early," he said. "Ground's like iron."

I said I'd brought compost. He asked to see. I lifted the bucket out and he looked in it, poked it with one finger, and rubbed the finger against his thumb. "That's not compost," he said, "that's peat and hope. What did you give for it?" I told him. He shook his head, slowly, the way a man does at a funeral for someone he didn't like.

The soil was as he said, hard on top and just giving underneath. I got the fork in with my foot and levered, and each forkful came up in a slab and I broke it with the back of the tine. It was cold enough to feel in the knuckles through the glove. Where the sun had got to it, the top inch went dark and soft, and the smell came up, iron and wet stone. I dug a trench along the north side, the length of six paces, and put the compost in it, and the sprouted potatoes I set out on the bench outside the shed in their egg boxes to harden in the air. I didn't plant. It is too early and Reg would have told me so, and I'd have gone home and done it out of pride.

I sat on the upturned bucket by the shed with the tea from the flask, the door held shut with its bootlace. The sun had got into the plot by then and my face was warm and my hands cold, which is how it goes in March. A wood pigeon came down on the water butt and considered me. Down the row, Reg was talking to another man about the state of the drains, and I heard him say, "You can't hurry a wet spring, you can only wait for it to be a dry one." It's the sort of thing he says. He says it like he's just thought of it.

I thought I might go and see about the Octavia's tyres. I thought about Wednesday, ten past four, and whether Marta would let me go, and how the day would have to be arranged round it, the bearing for the morning, the wash after. And I thought I should ring Nula, she'd not be expecting it on a Saturday, and it would give her a turn, and she'd say, "Who's dead?" I had my hand in my coat pocket on the phone. I sat there with it. The sun moved along the shed roof by about the width of a plank.
