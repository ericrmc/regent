Twenty to six, and no frost. The window was just wet, a thin grey wet with the streetlamp still in it, and I lay a minute working out what was different and it was the sound. Rain had been in the gutter in the night and gone. Bosun on my shins, Wafer on the pillow, Pilsudski on the third stair with his paws tucked in like a loaf. I carried the dog down past him and Pilsudski turned his head an inch, no more, the way a man at a bus stop turns it for a bus that isn't his.

Tea, thumb on the chip. Dry toast, the jar being finished, and I did not run the knife round it again. I filled the new thermos while the kettle was still loud, warmed it, emptied it, filled it, pushed the stopper in until it squeaked. The old green one was still by the bread bin where I'd left it on Sunday. I looked at it and did not pick it up. I put it in the cupboard under the sink, behind the bleach, which is not the bin and is not the shelf, and shut the door. The door shut. That pleased me more than it had any right to.

Walked in. The pavements had gone back to being pavements, black and shining, and my breath had nothing to show. On the Plain the deli was open and the woman was standing in the door with her arms folded looking at the sky as if she'd ordered something different. "Piotr," she said. I said good morning. "I've a jar," she said, "when you want it," and went in before I could say whether I did. I stood there like a man who's been handed a parcel and told not to open it.

At Voisey's the yard was full of Dennis Pym's cough. He was in early, wearing the black tie for no reason, and he took my hand in both of his and said, "Piotr, Piotr, how are you keeping?" and I said I was keeping, and he said, "Good man, good man," and let go, and that was the whole of it, as it always is. He smelled of Polos and the inside of a car. I went up to do the diary with my hand still feeling held.

I rang the vet from the middle landing at ten, under the light, with my back to the cream wall. I had it written on the back of my hand in biro, Wed 4.10, as if I might forget the one thing I have thought about all week. A girl answered, very bright, and I gave the name and she said "Oh, Bosun, yes, the ears and the lump," and I said the lump was not to be spoken of on a Tuesday, and she laughed, which I hadn't meant. Then she went quiet and I heard keys. "I'm so sorry, Mr Mahon, Miss Adeyemi's been called to a horse, she's not in on Wednesday afternoon at all. I can do Monday. Monday at eleven?" I said Monday I had a funeral in the morning, and she said, "Would it be tight?" and I said it might be, and she said she'd put it in pencil and someone would ring to confirm. Ring me where, I said. She said at home, the number on the file, and I said I was not at home, I was at work, and she said the number on the file was the home one. I gave her the work one. She read it back wrong and I corrected it, and she read it back wrong the same way. The light flickered over me. I said thank you, and she said, "Bye for now, bye, bye," and was gone, like something down a drain.

Then I had to go and tell Marta, who had put a pencil line through her whole Wednesday for me. She was in the arrangements office with the door ajar and a woman's voice coming out of the phone in her hand, so I stood outside and read the spines on the shelf, the little brass plate on the wall that says who we are. When she came off she said, "It's moved, hasn't it? I can see it on you." I said Monday. She said, "Oh, Piotr. Monday's the Trewin. It'll be tight." I said I knew it. She said, "Do you want me to ring them?" and I said no, they'd ring, and she said, "Will they?" and there was a small pause that had something in it I didn't like, and I said they would. She wrote something down. I do not know what she wrote. I said Wednesday I'd be in, then, and she said, "You'll be in, and you'll go home at half three anyway, and I'll tell Alan it's a bereavement," and I said it wasn't a bereavement, it was a beagle, and she laughed and I did not. I went out sooner than I needed to.

The whole day after that was odd, loose. Nothing to hold on to. A removal from Stoke, a man of ninety in a bed by a window where somebody had been sitting all night, the chair still warm, the cup on the sill with a skin on the tea. His son wanted to wash him and I said of course, and brought a bowl, and stood by the door. He did it well. He did the hands last. I looked at the shoes lined under the radiator, brown lace-ups polished to a mirror, and I thought, somebody polished those for a Sunday that didn't come. I said nothing. The son said, "He'd have wanted the black ones," and I said, "These are lovely," and he looked at them and something loosened.

At twelve I sat on the landing with the sausage roll and the new thermos, and poured, and steam came up out of the cup and fogged my glasses. It was hot. It was hot at noon. I held the cup in both hands like a child and drank it too fast and burned my lip and did not care. I turned the thermos round and looked at the dark blue of it, and the eight ninety-nine sticker still on the base, which I had forgotten to peel. I picked at it with a nail. It came off in a long strip and left a tacky ghost, and I rubbed that with my thumb until my thumb was grey.

Curious what I did next, and I still can't say why. I took the Derriford card out of my breast pocket and looked at it under the flickering light. Mrs D. Pascoe. Tuesday 2nd April, 9.40. There was a number on the back, in a different biro, smudged, and it might have been a ward or it might have been hers. I read it over four times. I thought, I could ring it. And what would I say, that I have your card, that I am a man who works with the dead and I have your card? I put it back. I do not know what it is about a small thing that belongs to someone else. It will not leave me alone. Kieran came past on his way down, quick on the treads, said "All right, Piotr," and I said all right, and he looked at the card in my hand and then away, politely, and went on, and the fire door took his shoulder and gave. I noticed that I had covered the card with my palm. I noticed it after.

Finished at four. It was going to be a light evening and I had had it in my head to go and put the compost on the plot, since I'd had the bags in the boot since Saturday, so I drove the Octavia round by Marsh Mills and left it in the lay-by and took a bag on my shoulder to walk the last of the way by the towpath. It's not the way. It's longer. But the track up to the Ridgeway gate is a stream this month and I'd sooner go along the water on the flat.

The towpath on that stretch is cinder and mud, and the river was full and brown and fast, the tide coming up under the bridge, and the mud banks shining. Smell of it, salt and rot and diesel, and something sweet from the buddleia stumps. There are boats moored along there, a row of them with their washing out, two with smoke going up from a tin chimney, one with a bicycle lashed to the roof and a cat in the window looking at me the way Pilsudski does. The bag on my shoulder was heavy and damp and had a corner pushing into my neck, the bad side, so I shifted it, and shifted it again.

Then the hail. It came out of nothing, out of a sky that had been only grey, a rattle in the trees first and then all of it at once, white grit bouncing off the cinders and stinging my ears and the back of my hands. I put my head down and stood there with the bag on me. It hit the river and the river hissed. Ninety seconds, I'd say. Not more. I counted out of habit, the way you count with a hymn. Then it thinned and stopped and the light came yellow under the cloud, and every twig had a bead of ice on it, and the path was speckled like a thrush.

It was when I put the bag down to get the crick out of my neck that it went. I had it on the edge of the path, on its side, and I let it go a bit hard, and the seam at the bottom gave with a long soft rip like a sheet, and the compost came out onto the cinders in one slow black slide. Wet and rich. It steamed. It smelled of the inside of the earth, of a cellar and mushrooms and the underneath of a log, a smell I'd have bottled. I stood over it with my two hands hanging. I tried to scoop what I could back into the bag but the bag was a bag with no bottom, and I ended up with my sleeves black to the elbow and about a third of it in and the rest lying in the hail on the towpath like something I'd killed.

There were two men on the boat nearest, sitting up on the stern in oilskins, an older one with a white beard cut short and a lad with a hat pulled down, and they had been watching me the whole time with a mug each. "Lost your bottom," said the old one. I said I had. "Have a bucket?" the lad said, and went below and came up with a green plastic bucket with a crack in the rim, and I scooped the compost into it with my hands, and he held it steady. The old man had a laminated sheet on his knee, held down with his thumb against the wind, and he was reading out of it in a flat voice, the way you'd read to a child who needs to learn it. "A ship under way gives way to a ship constrained by her draught," he read, and looked up at the lad. "See? It isn't who's got the right of way. It's who can do something about it. She can't turn. You can." The lad said, "But I've got the right of way, though, going up." "Not if she's deep," said the old man, and tapped the sheet. "You give way to what can't." The lad said, "That's not fair," and the old man said, "It's not meant to be fair, it's meant to keep you off the mud." They looked at me, and the lad said, "You all right with that?" and I said I was, and thanked them for the bucket, and said I'd bring it back. "Do," said the old man. "It's the only one."

I carried the bucket up the last of the path in one hand and the burst bag folded under my arm, and the handle bit into my fingers, and the compost lay in it dark and warm in the cold. At the plot the gate stuck and I had to lift it. Reg was not there. I put the compost on the beans bed in three heaps and forked it out and it lay on the cold ground looking like it had always been there. The shed door was held shut with the bootlace. I sat in the doorway a minute, with the smell of creosote and old string, and thought that I would take the bucket back on Thursday, and thought of the long way down the towpath, and then thought I might not. I did not want to be somebody with someone else's bucket, and then I remembered.

The loppers. Coming back along the road to the car with the burst bag I thought of them, my long-handled ones, the good red ones with the rubber grips, that I'd handed over the fence to Barry on the second of March because he said his own were blunt, and that he was going to do just the one branch. Three weeks. I'd seen them Sunday, I realised, leaning on the back of his shed under the guttering with the blades out in the rain, when I went out to see the frost. I had seen them and thought nothing. Now I thought a good deal. I would not go and ask. I would stand at the fence with my hands in my pockets on a Saturday and see whether he came out. I will not knock, I said aloud, in the car, to the windscreen.

Home at six. The light was out of the hall and the house was cold, and I went straight in and put on the gas fire and heard it tick and catch. I did two eggs in the pan, and did not sit. I ate them off the fork standing at the counter with the pan in my other hand, salt, pepper, the yolk going soft on the bread, Bosun's chin on my foot, Wafer on the fridge with her eyes half shut. I had the compost still under my nails. I ate the last bit off the pan and ran the pan under the tap and it hissed.

Then I went into the hall for the post. The machine on the shelf by the pig was blinking, one red, steady, the same as yesterday, or a different one. I stood in front of it with a tea towel over my shoulder. It might be the vet. It might be Roisin, who said she'd ring. It might be nobody.
