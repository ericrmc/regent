Twenty to six, and the window was a blank. Not dark, not light. The fog had come in off the Sound in the night and put itself against the glass like a hand. I lay and listened for the gutter and there was nothing, only the very slow tick of the gas fire cooling downstairs, which I hear on Sundays because nothing else is going. Bosun was on my shins. Wafer was on the pillow with her back to me. Pilsudski was on the third stair, a grey shape in the grey, and I carried the dog down past him and felt the cat's eyes on the back of my neck, on the new bare place. It is going to take a week to stop feeling the air there.

Tea. Then I filled the thermos, because I had it in my head to go in and do the hearse and the two cars ahead of Monday. Marta had said there was no need on a Sunday and I had said it wouldn't take an hour. The thermos is the old green one my father had in his tin box for the dockyard, dented at the shoulder, and it has not held heat since Christmas. I know this. I filled it with boiling water first, to warm it, as you do, and stood watching the steam go up, and then put the tea in and screwed the cup on and held it against my chest. Cool already. Not cold, worse than cold. I unscrewed the stopper and looked at the inside, at the silver that should be silver, and gave it a shake by my ear, and heard the small loose glassy noise of something gone in the wall of it. I have shaken it by my ear before. I put it on the side by the bread bin and thought, that's that, then, and took it anyway.

The road to Mutley was all headlamps and no shapes. I walked, because the Octavia had a frost on it and I did not want to stand and scrape. The fog was that thick white kind that doesn't move, that holds the sound close, so that a woman's heels forty yards away seemed to be in my own pocket. Wet on the eyelashes. The deli was shut, the grille down, and I stood a moment at it and thought of the jar with its smear at the bottom, and went on.

The yard was empty. I let myself in by the side door and the garage smelled of cold rubber and wax and the faint sweet chemical smell that comes up the stairs from the prep room and gets into everything cream-painted. The back stairs on a Sunday are a different place. No feet on them, no phone going. The strip light on the middle landing did its flicker, the on, off, on of it, and I stood under it with my hand on the rail, the shiny place, and let it. The fog had got in even there. The concrete at the bottom was beaded, and the fire door, when I put my shoulder to it, came off its frame with the usual shudder and I stood holding it and thought I might see about that. Not today. It has stuck for as long as I have been here and I have never once thought how to fix it, only how to lean.

The hearse first, because it takes longest. Leather on the chrome, the long cloth down the flanks, the black paint coming up under the wet like a pond. I did the wheels with the small brush, all the spokes, the tyre walls, and the fog settled on the roof as fast as I dried it, so that I was polishing a thing that was being un-polished at the same time. I gave it up around the bonnet and did the inside instead, the rollers in the back, the little brass fittings, the floor. Nobody about. Somewhere over towards the Plain a bus went by with its engine sounding like it was in another postcode.

At eleven I sat on the middle landing with the sausage roll from the Greggs I'd passed on the way, still warm from the bag, and the thermos on the step beside me. I poured the tea out into the cup. It was the temperature of a cup of tea that has been thought about. I drank it anyway, because I'd made it, and the sausage roll made flakes all down my front, which I picked off one at a time and put in the bag, out of manners to no one. The light flickered. Above me a door somewhere, then nothing. I thought I heard the phone in the office, the first floor, the one we take home on the switch after five, and stopped chewing to listen, and it was the pipes.

There is a smell in that stairwell in fog that I have never got used to. Wet and cream paint and something that is not in either. I sat in it for a good while longer than the sausage roll took. I could have gone back down to the cars. I looked at my hands, the knuckles, the grease of the wax pressed into the creases. I thought about Wednesday, ten past four. I said it once to the light. Then I got up and did the two Mercedes, and made a bad job of the second, and left it.

I did not go home. That was the thing. By one it was clear to me that at three the phone would go in the hall, and I would be standing there with my hands full of nothing, and I found I had turned left out of the yard instead of right. So I walked. Down through Mutley and Greenbank, along by the station and down towards the Hoe, everything shut and dripping, and I could not see the Sound. I heard it. The foghorn out at the Breakwater gave its long low note every half a minute and the whole of the city seemed to lean toward it. On the Hoe the grass was drowned and Smeaton's Tower a red and white stump going up into nothing. There were a few people, a man with a Labrador in a coat, two lads on bikes who came past out of the whiteness with no lights and were gone. I stood at the rail where the Sound should be. Grey and white, the same, top and bottom, no line. I thought that I could stand there and be at the end of a place.

I went down to the Barbican, and along the front, and up the steps past the fish market and round again, and by the time I had my feet on the road home I had been walking for four hours and my neck was pulling and the soles of my feet were on fire. The thermos knocked at my hip all the way in the bag like a small dead thing. Twice I nearly threw it in a bin. Twice I did not, and could not have said why, except that the bin was wet and it was his.

Home at ten to six. The hall was dark. The phone sat on its shelf by the pig, looking like nothing had happened. I did not check it, and I did not look at it, and I put my hand on the pig, on the brown envelope under it, and moved on.

The animals had all gathered in the kitchen like a committee. I did the dishes, all four. Then I took the cupboard door, the one that hangs a quarter inch proud, and I got the screwdriver from the drawer and the box of matches from over the cooker. The trick is that the hole in the carcass has gone soft, that the screw won't bite, so you break a match, two, three, and glue them in and let them dry. Nula's husband showed me it, years ago, over a cup of tea in a kitchen off Union Street, a man with lovely hands. I got the door down and laid it on the counter with its hinge showing. I dug out the old screw holes with the point of the screwdriver, and blew the dust, and there was the yellow of the old glue in one of them, from the last time I did this, so it must be a thing I do and forget I do.
