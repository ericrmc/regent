Twenty to six, and the window was clear. It was clear all the way to the fence, and the frost had drawn a white line along the top of every paling. I could see the hedge as separate leaves. After two days of nothing having a far side, it was a bit much. I lay and looked at it. Bosun was across my shins. I turned my head on the pillow, left ear down, and heard the house tick, the pipes coming up, and turned back.

The cupboard by the cooker was shut. I stood and looked at it. The beer mat was under the cooker where I'd kicked it, so it couldn't have been the beer mat. I put a finger on the edge and it swung out an inch, slowly, as if it had only been resting its eyes. I pushed it to and it stayed a moment and then didn't. I left it. The light was coming straight in the kitchen window, low, and it lit every smear on the door and every crumb on the lino. I found the bag of compost by the back door then, where I'd put it on Wednesday night in the fog, and I picked it up to move it and the seam went.

It went all down one side with a sound like a zip. Forty litres, from the garden centre on the Tavistock road, and I'd carried it in from the van on my shoulder and never looked at the stitching. It came out on the lino in a slow black tongue, damp, with bits of bark in it and one white worm of something. The smell got up at once, sweet and cold and a bit like a cellar. I stood holding the empty top half of the bag like a man who's been handed a coat. Wafer came and sniffed at the edge of it and walked off with her tail up. Pilsudski watched from the third stair. Nobody was going to help.

I got the dustpan and the coal shovel from the yard and did it on my knees. It is a thing you can't hurry. The compost is fine at the top and lumpy underneath, and it goes into the joins of the lino and won't come out. I put it in the washing-up bowl, the same one from yesterday, and then in the bucket, and it took four trips. I kept some back in a cup to put in the window box. I don't know why. My hands were black to the wrist and smelled of the good part of a garden, and the tea went cold on the side while I did it, and I let it.

Then Barry started up next door.

I thought at first it was a lorry reversing on the lane. It was a drill, though, a proper hammer drill, on and off, on and off, with long gaps between. It comes through the party wall as a kind of shudder in the plaster, and the cups on the rack answered it. Seven o'clock, near enough. I thought, he's got his eye, he's fixing something he couldn't see before. Or he's drilling into my fence. I went and stood at the back door with my black hands held out in front of me like a surgeon's and looked at the fence and there was nobody at it. The drill stopped. Then a long pause, and a man's voice saying something short and flat to nobody, and then it started again. I had a screwdriver in the drawer and I had the screws for the hinge, and I thought, well, there's a man with a proper drill. It didn't seem the moment. It never does.

The washing machine man rang as I was washing my hands. He had a young, hoarse voice, and the mother had told him to. He said he was back from Brixham sooner than he'd thought and could come Monday, between eight and twelve, instead of the end of the month. I said that would be fine, and that I'd be at work, and would leave the key with next door. He said no, he needed somebody in, it was a job where you'd want to talk. I said I'd get somebody in. He said, or the Tuesday, eight to twelve, and I said, Monday. When he'd gone I stood with the phone against my chest and the water running, and thought, who. Denny, maybe. Not Barry. Not with the eye.

The plan was for after work. Reg had said on Sunday, over the wire, that if I came up on the Friday he'd bring the barrow and we'd get the manure onto the bean trench before the ground went hard again, and that he'd put the kettle on the little stove in his shed. I'd said half past four. At ten to twelve, in the yard, while I was tipping the last of the bucket water out of the Mercedes' roof drain, Denny came across with his phone held out and said, "Some old boy for you." It was Reg's daughter. She said her dad wasn't up to it, it was his knee again, and he'd told her to say he was sorry, and it'd keep till Saturday. I said it would. I said to tell him not to lift anything. She said, "Try telling him," and laughed, and then didn't, and then said, "Thanks, Mr Mahon," and went. I'd been going to say he could send the barrow. I hadn't thought of it till she'd put the phone down.

So the afternoon had a hole in it, and I didn't fill it. We had a funeral at two, a small one, a woman of ninety-four from a home in Mannamead, six people and a vicar with a cold. The light was so hard and clear that every brass handle threw a star on the ceiling of the hearse. It was a short service. The vicar sneezed into his sleeve at the committal and apologised to the coffin. One of the six, a nephew, stood at the back with his hands in his pockets the whole way through and I thought he would go before the end, and he didn't, and afterwards he shook my hand and said, "You're the only one who looked at us." I didn't know what to do with that. I said it was cold. He said it was.

At half past three I was in the stairwell with the sausage roll I hadn't eaten at dinner. It had gone slightly leathery in the paper bag. The strip light on the middle landing was on its bad rhythm and I ate it under that, with my shoulder against the cream wall, and it went into me in three bites like something posted. Somebody was hoovering the office above. The whole stairwell hummed. Behind the fire door the prep room fan was going, and the smell that comes up from there, which I don't smell any more except when I've been away a day.

I drove home the long way, by the front, with the sun going down orange behind the Hoe and the sea flat and hard as a plate. It's a shame to see a day like that at five o'clock, from a van. Every window on the Barbican was on fire. I stopped by the wall at Millbay and got out and stood with my hands in my armpits and there was no wind at all. Two gulls were on a bollard, not speaking. I thought I'd ring Nula from here, the light was so good, and I dialled and stood with the crack in the screen catching the sun in a thin white line.

It rang out. I let it go to fourteen. It's a habit now, counting. Then a tone, and a woman I didn't know said the number was not available at present and to try later, in the voice they use, and I closed it. I thought, they've turned the phone off. The warden has. Or she's put it in the drawer. I stood a while longer. A man went by with a Labrador in a fluorescent coat and said, "Lovely," meaning the evening, and I said it was.

When I got in the kitchen was full of low gold light, right to the back wall, and there was the fine black dust I'd missed on the lino, drawn out into little tracks by the cats. I got the brush again. Then I got the screwdriver and the box of screws and pulled the cupboard door right off and laid it on the table. The chipboard round the hinge plate had gone to the texture of stale cake. Where the screw had been there was a hole the size of my little finger, with crumbs in it. I put my thumb in it. A screw wouldn't bite in that, not the long ones, not any. You'd want to glue in some matchsticks, or a dowel, and let it set overnight, and I had no glue that wasn't the dried-up tube from the shed. I stood with the door in my hands, and Bosun came and put his nose against the side of my knee and looked up at me, and then at the door, and then at the stairs, where Pilsudski was. I carried the door to the hall and leaned it against the wall by the phone book, next to the letter, and it looked like something waiting for a lift.

The cupboard stood open, with the tins on show. Beans, tomatoes, three of the little pink fish, a tin of the pears Mum liked. I looked at that for a good while, not doing anything about it. Then I put the kettle on and took down Barry's green flask, and filled it, and stood with the lid in my hand, waiting for it to be warm enough to say I'd tried it. The drill next door had started again.
