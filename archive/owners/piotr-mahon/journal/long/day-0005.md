# 2019-03-08

Twenty to six, and the frost had done the whole garden. Not a dusting. It was thick and furred, and it had got on to the shed roof and the lid of the water butt and the wire on Barry's side. The sky over the roofs was the colour of a knife. I had my tea at the sink, standing, and the window went from grey to pink to plain bright while I drank it, and the white didn't shift at all.

The phone on the table had a green light on it, the one I hadn't looked at last night. I looked at it now. It was Chloe from the surgery: Confirming Monday 11.00 for Bosun, please arrive 10 min early. Two lines and a smiley. I don't know what I had thought it was.

I opened the cupboard door and shut it. Opened it, shut it. It hung true. The tins stood in their rows behind it, and Wafer came and sat in front of it and looked up at me as if I'd put a wall in her way. Bosun went down the hall along the skirting and Pilsudski, on the third stair, opened one eye and shut it again.

The windscreen of the Octavia was a sheet of grey lace. I scraped it with an old library card I keep in the door, in long pulls, and the shavings of ice went down my cuff. The compost in the boot was hard as a sack of gravel when I put my hand on it. It didn't slump any more and it didn't sigh. It was frozen through, and I took my hand off it quickly and did not finish the thought.

Mrs Rudd was at half past eleven at Efford. The frost had gone off the roads by then but not off the yard, and the hearse roof had a skin of it that the sun hadn't reached. Kieran was out there before me with a bucket of warm water from the tap by the garage. I nearly told him not to, warm water on cold glass, and I watched him instead. He poured it over the roof in a slow sheet, not the windscreen, and wiped after with the chamois, and it worked. It steamed for a moment and then it was just clean black glass. He looked round and said, "Bit of a trick my nan does," and I said, "Good," and I meant it, and I could feel myself watching him do the wing mirrors. I don't know for what. He did them well.

Marta had the daughter in the front room by ten, a woman of about fifty in a camel coat with the collar up as if she were still outside. Beside her sat an old man, the brother, with a walking stick between his knees and both hands on top of it. He looked all wrong on the sofa, knees higher than his hips, and I went and got one of the upright chairs from the arrangements room and set it beside him and said, "This may be easier on the back, sir," and he looked at me as though I'd offered him a drink. Then he moved across and sat down straight. The daughter said, "She'd have hated this cold. She always said March was a trick," and nobody had an answer to that, and Marta said, "It is," quietly, and that was right.

Four of us carried her. It was a light one, and the frost had come back into the shade of the crematorium porch, and the path was slick under the gravel. I kept my eyes on the heel of the man in front. The old man came behind on his stick, very slow, and I fell back half a pace at the corner without looking round. He didn't say anything, but his stick went down more firmly. Inside it smelled of chrysanthemums and a heater that had been on since seven. They played something with a cello. The daughter didn't cry until the curtain, and then not much, and Marta stood behind her with her hands folded and her face doing nothing at all, which is the hard part.

Back at the yard at two, hungry, and a queue at Greggs, so I walked instead. The hospice shop on the Plain has a window I never look in, and I looked in it. In the corner behind a set of egg cups and a brass ship's bell was a green steel flask. A Stanley, the proper old shape, with a cup for a lid and a little scuff on the shoulder but not a dent in it anywhere. Four pounds. The woman behind the counter, in a cardigan the colour of porridge, said, "It was a gentleman's, we've a lot of his, it was a shed clearance," and handed it to me like a sleeping thing. I unscrewed it and looked inside. The steel was bright. I held it up to my ear, which is a daft thing to do, and it made that dead, thick, well-made silence. I took it back to the yard and filled it from the kettle in the garage and stood it on the bench and at four put my palm on it. Cold as a step. I stood there with my hand on it like a man taking a pulse.

My father's flask is in the drawer with the string and the batteries. I couldn't put it in the bin.

Six, the house dark and the dog looking at me. I'd stripped the duvet off the bed that morning without thinking, and the two dog beds and the blanket the cats use on the chair, and none of it would go in the machine. It never had. So, into the blue crate, then into the Octavia in a black bag, and off to the launderette on Alma Road, which stays open till eight and has done since before I bought the house. The bell on the door is a real one, on a spring. It was hot in there. A sweet, flat, burnt smell of lint and powder, and the tumble dryers going round in their round windows like the eyes of something big and patient. A woman in a tabard was folding sheets at the counter with the corners in her teeth. Nobody else but a lad asleep with his hood up on the bench under the notices.

I put in two washes, the beds and the duvet. Bosun's bed came out of the bag stiff and smelling like him, which is to say like a warm biscuit gone off, and I pushed it into the drum with a flat hand. Then I put the money in and sat, and it was too hot to keep my coat on, so I took it off, and something slid out of the inside pocket and lay on the lino.

A hospital appointment card, small and stiff and blue-grey. Derriford. Audiology, Level 2. Tuesday 12 March, 9.40. Please bring this card with you. I had put it in the coat in February and told no one, not even the dog. The GP had said, after I said pardon for the third time, that it was probably nothing, that I should get it looked at. I said I would. It had the same shine to it as a bus ticket. I hadn't said a word to Marta, and Tuesday I'm probably down for a removal. I turned it over. The back was blank. I put it in my shirt pocket, not the coat, because a shirt goes in the wash and I'd see it.

The drum turned. It's a good sound, a wet slap and a roll, a slap and a roll. I watched the duvet go round in it, a pale mass that rose and fell, and it was then, plainly, with no warning, that I thought: it's the eighth. And then: Wednesday was the sixth.

Roisin's birthday. Two days. I sat there. In eleven years I never missed it, and in six since, I had sent a card every time, through her door or in the post, always a couple of days early, and now I hadn't so much as thought of it. The gutters, the ladder, the dentist. All of that I had remembered. It was in my head under the wrong date, or under no date.

I rang her from the plastic chair. It rang six times and I nearly went, and then she said, "Piotr," in the voice she has for the phone, level and a little wary.

"Many happy returns," I said. "Two days late. I'm sorry."

"Oh," she said. There was a pause with a television in it. "Well. You're not the first. My sister rang yesterday for the one on the sixth. She got it mixed up with Mam's."

"I'll get a card."

"You don't have to get a card."

"I'll get one anyway."

She laughed a bit at that, through her nose, which I hadn't heard in a long time. She asked where I was, because of the noise, and I told her, the launderette, the duvet, and she said, "Still can't get it in the machine," and I said no, and she said she'd told me to buy a smaller one in 2011. That was true. She had. I looked at the drum going round and round and I was about to ask how the day had been when she said, "Hang on, Piotr, there's someone," and I heard the phone go down on something hard, a table or a shelf. Steps. A door, a long way off. A murmur, one voice, then two.

The dryer next to me finished with a buzz. The woman in the tabard shook out a sheet. I held the phone against my good ear, and the other one went on doing what it does, a soft fizz behind everything, and I couldn't make anything of the murmuring. It went on. A minute, two. Then a tone, and the line was empty.

I looked at the screen. Call ended, four minutes eleven. I didn't ring back.
