Twenty to six, and the window was white. Not the clear frost of Saturday. This was hard, thick, furred on the glass in ferns, so that I had to lie with my head on the pillow and look through the one clear place in the corner where my breath had been. Bosun was across my shins. The pipes knocked once, and then twice, and stopped. I thought that at some point the cold gets into the house and stays, and you can't tell any more whose it is.

The kitchen was the same as Sunday, but sharper. I put my hand flat on the cooker before I lit anything, to see how cold it was, and it was cold like a tombstone. Tea in the Argyle mug, thumb on the chip. The old green flask was still on the windowsill by the beer mat, with its rattle. I picked it up and shook it and put it down. I'd been going to do something about that.

The cupboard door was on the table where I'd left it. I had one screw to take, on the second dowel, and I'd stopped there. This morning I tried it with the light on. I held the door up to the frame and turned the driver and felt it bite, and go round, and bite again. Then I hung it on the one good hinge and let go. It stayed. I shut it. It stayed shut. I opened it and shut it again, three times, like a man testing ice. The third dowel I left alone. The tins were behind it now, the beans and the three pink fish and Mum's pears, and the cupboard looked the way a mouth looks when it has decided to keep something to itself. I didn't say anything about it to the cats.

The washer was still dead, and there was a pillowcase of it by the back door: sheets and two towels and four white shirts and the shirt I'd had on Friday. I couldn't wait for Tuesday. The one shirt smelled of the prep room under the soap smell, that sweetish clean smell that never goes out, and I didn't want to wear it to a family. So at ten to seven I put the pillowcase over my shoulder like a man delivering something and went out.

The frost had got everything. The parked cars were white to the door handles, and along Outland Road every hedge stood up furred and separate with its own small edge of light. My boots made a noise on the pavement I don't often hear, a crisp small crack at each step, like walking on sugar. A man scraping his windscreen with a bank card nodded at me and I nodded at him. Nobody talks to you on a frost. It's a sort of agreement. The sky was pale and hard over the roofs and there was no wind at all, and my breath went straight up.

The launderette is on the corner past the chemist, with a wire grille pulled halfway up and a light on inside. It was warm when I ducked under. It smelled of hot cotton, and powder, and the wet-dog smell that dryers make when they've been going all night, though they hadn't. A woman in a blue tabard was wiping the top of a machine with a cloth in long strokes and did not look up. I said good morning. She said, "Ten or fourteen?" and I said fourteen, and she said, "Number six, the seven's got a temper," and went on wiping.

I emptied the pillowcase on the folding table. The Friday shirt was on top, and I went through its pockets, because I always do, and in the breast one there was a card. Soft at the corners, with the crease across the middle where it had been folded to fit. Derriford Hospital. Audiology. Thursday 21st March, 10.20, filled in with a stranger's round hand, and my name misspelled, Mahone, with the e on the end that I've stopped minding. I'd forgotten I'd made it. January, I think. I'd told the girl on the phone that it was only a ringing, and she had said, "They all say that," not unkindly, and I had said I thought it was the left. I stood by the folding table with the card in my hand. Thursday, then. I'd have to say it at work, that I'd be out, and give a reason, and there would be somebody who said, "Oh, is everything all right?" and I would have to say something back.

I put it in my top pocket, which wasn't going to be washed, and then took it out again and put it in the pocket of the donkey jacket, which was hanging on the chair by the door. Then I loaded number six. The machine took the pound coins with a clunk that went right through the floor. I sat down on the orange bench with my back against the tiles and watched the sheets go round and round in the little round window, the grey light of them, and the suds coming up in a slow white sea, and I counted the turns, and lost count.

That was when I remembered it. The woman with the cloth had gone in the back, and a girl, a student, I'd say, had come in with a laundry bag bigger than she was and was folding a duvet cover into the smallest square she could, corner to corner, with her chin on the top. I watched her. She had a card sticking out of her coat pocket, a pink one, a big 18, and I thought, well, somebody's birthday. And it went through me. The fifteenth. It had been Friday. Roisin's, the fifteenth of March, and I had always known it, without trying, for eleven years and for six after that. It was Friday, and I had been in the chapel of rest with a lily in each hand, arguing with a florist over the phone about the ribbon. I had not thought of it. Not once. Then I'd gone home and had eggs.

I sat and looked at the sheets go round. I thought, she'll have had a nice day, and then I thought, she'll have had it with Graham, and then I thought, I don't know that there is a Graham, and that in fact I had no reason to say that name to myself at all. Where had I got it? I couldn't think where I'd got it. Somebody had said it on the ladder, I think, or on the step, some bit of it in passing, and now it was in my head, and it wasn't going.

There is a shop next to the launderette, a hardware and cards and bits, with a bell on the door and a smell of paraffin and rope. It opens at eight, and I was on the pavement with my hands in my armpits, at five to, when the man came and turned the sign. I said I'd be wanting a card. He said the cards were at the back. They were on a wire stand, three sorts, and I took the least bad, a plain one with a blue jug of something on it and nothing written in it. Then, because I was there, and because I'd been in my head about the flask since Sunday, I looked along the shelf, and there was a flask. A plastic one, cheap, with a red cap, the kind you'd have on a building site, and a label that said £4.49. I unscrewed the top, and it had a stopper inside, and I put my nose in it. It smelled of nothing. I bought it. I thought I'd have got a good deal more from it if it had been worse.

I wrote the card on the bench, with the shop's biro, while the dryer turned. I put, Many happy returns, sorry it's late. P. I looked at it, and I added, Hope the boiler's behaving. Then I scratched that out, because she hadn't said a word about a boiler, and I'd no way to know, and it wasn't a kindness, only a man wanting to be useful at a distance. I wrote it out again on the back, just Many happy returns, sorry it's late, and left off the P, since she'd know. Then I couldn't tell. I put a P.

The dryer took forty minutes. The woman folded my sheets for me without being asked, while I stood there, and I let her, and the frost outside was going off the roofs now, in a slow line, the way it does from the sun side first. It was gone from the road by ten, and lay only in the shadow of the wall, grey and wet at its edge. I posted the card in the box on the corner. It made a small dry sound going down.

At the yard I filled the flask from the urn in the garage, and stood it on the bench by the hearse cloths. At noon I sat on the stairs with the sausage roll and poured a cup, and it was hot. Properly hot, so that I had to blow on it. I sat with the steam going up in the cold stairwell, past the flickering light on the middle landing, and I thought, four forty-nine. I couldn't get over it.
