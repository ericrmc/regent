Twenty to six. Tea, thumb on the chip. The cupboard under the sink had swung open in the night and the beer mat I keep folded under the door was on the lino again, face up, a Bass one gone soft at the corners. I put it back. It will be on the floor tomorrow, and I know that, and I put it back anyway. The hinge wants a new screw and a bit of wood in the hole, and I have both of those things somewhere. I stood with the mat in my hand and looked at the cupboard, and the cupboard stayed open a finger's width, as it does, and let out its smell of bleach and old cloth.

Bosun was at the back door with his nose on the gap. Pilsudski had the third stair. I carried the dog down past him again, and I have stopped thinking of it as a thing I do. It is only how the morning goes.

At ten past eight someone hammered the blue door as if the house owed him. I had my shirt half buttoned. A lad in a yellow jacket, a van running at the kerb with its hazards going, a parcel the size of a shoebox held out in front of him like something hot. "Coombe?" he said. I said no. "Forty-one?" I said this was not forty-one, and he looked at the door, then at his machine, and turned it round to show me, as if I could read it. "It says here it's this one." I said Mr Coombe was next door but one, a different road altogether, and that I thought forty-one was Wilton Street, down the bottom, past the chapel. He said, "Are you sure?" I said I had lived here seven years. "Right," he said. "Cheers, mate. It's this bloody thing." He shook the machine. He went off down the path with the parcel under his arm, and I heard him say something to the van, and it pulled away and left the road quiet with a smell of diesel. I thought, Barry will have a parcel, and I wondered what he orders. I did not wonder for long.

The letter was on the mat under where the flap had opened. A brown envelope with a window and a franking mark, Derriford in the corner, my name typed with the Mahon spelt right, which they don't always. I did not open it. I put it in my inside pocket and it sat against my ribs all through the first hour, the way a card does, or a hand.

The ten o'clock was cancelled. The family had moved it to Monday, a cousin coming from Leeds, and Marta wrote it on the board in red and drew a line through the old slot. So I had until half past twelve. I said I would go up to Derriford. She said, "Physio?" and I said no, only a letter, and she said, "Take the Corsa," and I said I didn't have the Corsa, I had the Octavia, and she looked at me a moment and said, "I meant the van, sorry, I've been on the phone since seven." I took the Octavia.

The multi-storey was half empty for a Friday. I went up to Level 6 out of habit, round and round the ramp with the tyres squealing on the painted arrows, and parked by the barrier where the gulls stand. The machine takes coins in the slot on the left, if you hold the flap. I did not need the machine. I sat in the car with the engine off and the window down two inches and took out the envelope and opened it with my thumb.

It was a new time. That was all it was. A Tuesday in April, twenty to ten, and a paragraph telling me to arrive fifteen minutes early and to wear loose clothing, and a second sheet, folded in three, that was a map of the site with a red arrow and the word YOU. I read it twice. I do not know why I had thought it would be more. There was a little printed box at the foot that said if this appointment is not convenient, and a number, and I looked at the number and put the letter on the passenger seat face down.

Then the hail. It came across from the Tamar side with no warning, a grey wall, and struck the roof of the car so hard I said something aloud. It bounced on the bonnet in white beads and ran along the ledge by the barrier, and the gulls all went up at once, complaining, and hung over the ramp with their feet down. The noise was tremendous. Ninety seconds, perhaps, I did not time it, and then it ceased as if it had been turned off at the wall, and the beads lay on the concrete with the light coming through them and began going to water as I watched. A man came out of the stairwell door with a carrier bag over his head, laughing, and stopped, and looked at the sky, and went back in. I found I was smiling. It is a fine thing to be under a roof for weather you did not ask for.

The ambulances went by on the road below, one and then another, no siren on the second. I sat another ten minutes with the window down and the wet smell coming in, gravel and tar and something metal, and I watched a gull walk the length of the barrier and back as if it were inspecting it. I did not go in. I did not have a reason to, and it seemed a poor thing to go in without one.

The barber on the Plain is a Greek called Stelios who has cut my hair since 'ninety-nine and has never asked what I would like. He put the cloth round my neck and pulled it tight enough to make me swallow, and began. Two chairs, and the other one had a man in it about my age in a work fleece with a plaster on his thumb, talking to the mirror. He was telling Stelios about a barn. A frame, oak, that he had been helping put up for a fellow near Yelverton, all of it cut and fitted on the ground and then lifted, and he said the whole business turned on the pegs. "You bore the hole in the post, right, and then in the tenon you bore it again but you put it a shade nearer the shoulder, a quarter inch, so the peg's got to bend to go through, and as it goes in it drags the joint tight. That's a drawbore pin. So you're fixing it and clamping it in the one go, see. No wedges, nothing." Stelios said, "Mm." The man said, "Four hundred years, they'll stand." He turned his head to see how it looked and Stelios turned it back for him.

I kept my eyes on the man in the glass. I said nothing, as is my habit. But I thought of the shoehorn in the Peek Freans tin, and the hole in its end, and I could not say why. Stelios ran the clippers up the back of my neck and the cold went down my spine. The floor around the chair was covered in grey. I did not remember it being so grey.

"Bit off the top?" he said, as he does, and I said yes, as I do. He held up the little mirror at the back. I looked at the back of my own head. It looked like somebody else's, and older, and well kept.

The flower woman was not at the market as I passed it. I had an idea to go in for the whites for tomorrow, and stood in the door under the roof with the smell of onions and wet cardboard coming out at me, and then remembered it was Marta's job. I went back to the yard with the wind on my newly cut neck.

From half twelve it was the removal of a man from Stoke, eighty-eight, a bungalow with a stairlift and a photograph of a ship on every wall. His wife said, "He was in the Navy, you know," and I said I could see. She said, "He never talked about it." There was a chair in the hall and I brought it for her, and she sat on it with her handbag on her knees as if she were waiting for a bus, and I said we would be as quick and as gentle as we could. Kieran was at the head. He did it well. He always does the head well, and I watched his hands.

Dennis had turned up at the yard for the gossip and stood at the garage door with his cap in his fist. "Piotr, Piotr," he said, and took my hand, and this time held it a second longer, and I waited, and he said, "Nice haircut," and let go. I said thanks. He said, "Stelios?" and I said yes, and he said, "Good man," and looked at the Mercedes. That was all. I put my hand in my pocket. It was still warm.

At six I came in and the house was quiet and the light was going yellow along the top of the fence. I did not sit. There were four pierogi left in the bag, and I put them in the pan with the butter, and stood at the counter by the cooker with the fork and ate them out of the pan, one, two, three, the fourth going cold while I was looking at the cupboard. The door had come open again on its own. There was the beer mat, face up on the lino, and a little dust ring where it had been.
