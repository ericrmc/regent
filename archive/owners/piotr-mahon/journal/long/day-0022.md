Twenty to six, and the frost had done the whole of the window. Not a corner this time. Ferns of it, right across, and the glass so cold when I put my finger to it that it stuck a second. The light through it was white and grainy like the light in the prep room. Wafer had gone under the covers in the night and was a warm knot against the back of my knee. Bosun on my shins. Pilsudski on the third stair, fluffed to twice his size, and I carried the dog past him with my chin on the dog's head so I didn't have to meet anyone's eye.

Tea, thumb on the chip. Toast, and the last of the jar gone. I ran the knife round the glass twice and there was nothing to have, only the sweet dark smell of it, so I stood at the back door and had dry toast and the smell. The whole garden was white. Barry's fence, the top of the hedge, the washing line with each peg wearing its own little fur. Not a sound from next door, for once. I thought he was saving himself.

The cupboard door. I had left it overnight with the glue in the holes and the door propped against the wall, and this morning I put it on, screw by screw, and it took. It bit. I shut it and it stayed shut, flush with the frame, and I opened and shut it four times like a boy. It doesn't look like much, a door that shuts. I had my hand on it a good while.

I wore the good overcoat to work because of the cold and walked, the Octavia being a block of ice, and the pavements were like sugar. Everybody going along with their heads down and their breath going ahead of them. On Mutley Plain the deli was open, the grille up, the light on, and the woman was in the window doing something to a tray of rolls. She lifted a hand. I lifted mine. I did not go in, because I could not have said what I wanted, and I felt her look follow me down the road.

Monday at Voisey's is always the weekend's leftovers. Two removals, one from a house in Efford and one from Freedom Fields, and a family coming at eleven to see their mother. I did the Efford one with Dennis Pym, who was on the other end of the stretcher and breathing hard on the stairs, and who talked all the way down about the frost and his knee and a man he knew who'd slid on his own drive and broken his collarbone. A narrow landing, a turn, a bedroom with the curtains still drawn and a smell of Vicks and warm carpet. She was small. Somebody had put her slippers side by side under the chair. I looked at them for longer than the others would have noticed. Her daughter was on the stairs, in her coat, holding a mug she'd forgotten she had, and I said we'd take our time, and we did.

In the afternoon I set out the chapel for the eleven o'clock family, moved the chairs an inch, then moved them back, and opened the window to take the chill off and shut it again because that was worse. Then I went up to the office to see Marta about Wednesday. She was on the phone with her hand flat on a diary, and she held up a finger and I stood there, and I could hear the other end going on and on, some man with a lot to say about flowers. She put her hand over the receiver. "Wednesday? What time?" I said ten past four. "Is it the dog?" I said it was. She looked at me for a moment and wrote it in pencil. "Go at half three," she said. "I'll do the wash myself, I won't die." The man on the phone said something and she said, "Yes, yes, no, I'm still here," and turned her chair away, and that was that. I went down the back stairs with my hand on the rail. Kind, she is. I do not know why it puts me in a temper.

After work I went to the launderette on Outland Road, because the machine at home has not drained since Thursday and stands there with its mouth full of grey water like a sulking child. I had the duvet cover in a bin bag, and the dog's blanket, and the second blanket which is Wafer's and smells of Wafer. I had two bags of it against my chest, like a man carrying two babies out of a fire.

The smell is what I like about the place, warm cotton and powder and something hot and metallic coming off the dryers. And the windows steamed all down the inside so you can't see the road, and the strip light with a moth bumping against it. Two machines going, both with something pink. A young woman on the plastic chairs, with her feet up on a bag, on the phone, and I gathered from what she said that it was somebody's birthday. "Happy birthday to you," she sang, low and out of tune, then laughed and said, "No, I'm at the launderette, don't laugh, we've no hot water." I put my coins in, and the money went down the slot with a small heavy clunk. I stood and watched the drum fill, and the frost was still in my fingers.

Birthday. It went through me like cold water. It was Saturday. She'd said "Are you up?" and "Don't come, it'll be a madhouse," and it was her birthday, the twenty-third, her sister and the kids, and I had a sack of compost on the floor and the whole morning with nowhere to put it. Eleven years, and then six on top, and I had let it go by with a hundred jobs in my head. I stood with my hand on the glass of the machine, warm now, the water starting to go round with a sound like a slow breath. I thought of the breath she took on the phone. I thought that was what it had been.

I went out on the step with my mobile in the frost. The pavement was already white again where the sun had left it. It rang for a long time, and I counted, six, seven, eight, and I thought I would put it down, and she picked up on what must have been the last one. "Piotr," she said, and there was a room behind her, a lot of voices. "Can I ring you back? I've got people." I said that I'd only wanted to. I said, "It was Saturday," and she said, "What was?" and there was a shout, a child, a glass, and she said, "Sorry, love, I can't hear you, I'll ring you," and the line went to nothing. I looked at the little screen. I did not ring again. A man went by with a Yorkshire terrier in a red jacket and the dog looked at me with contempt.

When I went back in, the pink machines had stopped and the girl was gone, and on the ledge by the dryers there was a card. Somebody had left it in the drum, and it lay on the warm steel with its corner curled. I picked it up. A hospital appointment card, Derriford, the plain blue kind with the name in biro: Mrs D. Pascoe, Tuesday 2nd April, 9.40, Outpatients, Level 3. Nothing else on it. A ring of tea, or something, on the back. I turned it over twice. It had been through a hot wash, or nearly. I thought of her sitting on Tuesday week in some corridor with her hands in her lap, thinking she'd lost it, and whether they'd turn her away at the desk. I put it on the shelf by the soap machine where anyone looking would look, and then thought that was no good, and put it in my breast pocket instead. I did not know what I would do with it. I know how it goes with a name and a time. There is always somebody who comes back.

I put the duvet cover in the dryer for forty minutes. I sat down. There were four other people, none of us speaking, all looking at our own washing going round, as if it might do something. An old man in a donkey jacket sat with his arms folded and his eyes closed, and I watched him, in case. He wasn't. He was only resting.

On the way home, the shop by the crossroads that sells everything, the one with brooms hung outside on a chain, was still open, and I went in for a bulb. I came out with a thermos. I had not meant to. It was on the shelf by the door, a plain steel one, dark blue, with a cup that screws off the top and a stopper that goes in with a good squeak, and eight ninety-nine on a yellow sticker. The man said, "That's a good one, that. You keep it upright." I bought it and did not say what I had at home.

At the kitchen table I filled it from the kettle, then emptied it, then filled it again, as you do, and put the stopper in and left it. Bosun was in the hall on his worn place, watching the door. The bag of washing smelled of warm dust. On the shelf the little light on the machine was blinking, one red, and I stood and looked at it, and I did not press it.
