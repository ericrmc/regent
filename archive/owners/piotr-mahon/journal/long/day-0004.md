Twenty to six, and the fog was in. I knew before I opened the curtain, because the house sounded wrapped. The window over the sink was a white sheet with the shed floating in it, no garden, no fence, no Barry. The shed was a darker patch and nothing else. Tea in the Argyle mug, and I stood with both hands round it and watched nothing happen outside.

The cupboard door was still on the worktop. Thirty-six hours now on the matchsticks. I pressed a thumbnail into each one in turn and none of them gave. I decided to do it tonight, in the light, with the good screwdriver, when I could take my time. I don't know why I put it off. Wafer sat in front of the bare shelf again and looked at it like a cinema.

The thermos was the first job. It was my father's, a green steel Thermos with a dent in the base and a cup that screws on the top, and it has gone. It has been going since Christmas. You fill it at ten to six and by ten it's tea-coloured water at the temperature of a bath somebody else has used. I've been telling myself it's the stopper. So I took the stopper out and looked at the cork of it, which was fine, and I ran a thumb round the rim, which was fine, and I boiled the kettle again and filled it to the top and screwed it shut and stood it on the side and waited. Ten minutes. Then I put my palm flat on the outside of it. Just warm. Not hot. If the vacuum was sound the metal would be cold as a step, because the heat's meant to stay in. It was warm right through, like a cat that's been on a radiator. The dent, then. Con must have dropped it off a scaffold in about 1981 and it's been dying by inches ever since.

I tipped it out into the sink and the water steamed up into the fog on the window. I stood the flask on the draining board and said, right. That's that. I put it in the bag anyway, because there's nothing else to put tea in, and I am not the sort to buy a new one from Robert Dyas and stand there with the price on it.

The dog was at the back door with his nose on the crack. He wanted the garden. I opened it and the fog came in at the knees, wet and cold, with the taste of a cellar in it, and he went out, and I heard him stop. He had smelled something. Then a gap, and a noise from the other side of the fence, a cough, a man's one dry cough, and Bosun came back inside so fast he skidded on the lino. I shut the door.

I could not have said why I did what I did next. I put my coat on and my boots and took the lead off the hook and said, "Come on, then," and the dog looked at me in a way that meant he knew it was not his usual time and didn't like it. But he came. I went out the front. I didn't go through the back and down the passage, I went out the blue door and turned left instead of right, which took us the wrong way entirely, away from the Plain and away from the yard, and I kept going.

It was a long walk. Up Outland Road, across at the lights, into Central Park, where the fog sat on the grass and you could see about thirty yards of anything. A man in a bobble hat went past with a spaniel and was gone the moment he'd passed. The trees came up out of the white one by one, black and wet, and the path was slick under the leaves. Bosun walked at my heel with his head low. He's ten. There was a moment by the pond where he sat down and wouldn't get up, and I stood over him and looked at the water, which you couldn't see the far side of, and a moorhen went across it with a small noise like tearing paper. Then we went on, round the top of the park, out by the football ground, and down through the streets behind it. I went all the way round by Home Park. I knew what I was doing. I was putting the streets between me and the back garden, and the fence, and the thing with the nail that I hadn't looked at properly. It seems a lot of ground to cover to not look at a nail.

I carried him for the last bit up Alma Road. He didn't fight it. He put his chin over my shoulder and I could feel his breath cold and quick on my neck, on the bad side, and I walked with my head held very straight so as not to jar it.

I was twenty minutes late to the yard and nobody said. The fog had come down over Mutley Plain like a lid and the traffic went by in pairs of headlights with a smear of red behind. The deli was lit up, yellow, a warm square, and I did not stop. In the yard it dripped from everything. I did the hearse, which is a mistake in fog, because you wash it and the fog lands on it straight after and you've done nothing. I did it anyway. The chamois was cold and heavy and I wrung it every few strokes with my knuckles going white.

The stairwell at ten to one was as bad as I've known it. The fog had come in under the back door and up the stairs, or the smell of it had, that wet-concrete, wet-coat smell, and the strip light on the middle landing ticked and dimmed and ticked. I sat on the third step from the landing with the sausage roll in my lap. Above me Alan's voice, low, on the phone in the office, the door not quite shut. I couldn't hear the words, only the shape of it, the rise at the end of each sentence like a man asking for something politely. Then a pause. Then a laugh, one short one, not his real one. I ate the sausage roll. The pastry was cold. The flask went round in my hand and I unscrewed the cup and poured. Tepid, and a bit grey, a taste of the metal to it. I drank it all, because it was there.

Somebody came down from the office, Kieran, with his jacket over his arm, and said, "All right, Piotr," and I said, "All right," and he took the fire door at the bottom with his shoulder, first go, the way I never can. He did it without looking. It shut behind him with that long sucking noise and the fog came in at the gap.

The afternoon was one visit, a nursing home in Efford, a small woman, very light. Two of us and the trolley. The corridor smelled of gravy and floor polish. The nurse who signed for her said, "She was no trouble," which they all say, and I said, "No," which I always say. On the way back the fog had gone from white to the colour of a dirty sheet, and it was only four and lights were on all along the road.

Home, and the compost still in the boot. I put my hand on the lid of it and left it there. Tomorrow, or Saturday. Reg's knee.

I did the door after I'd fed them. Dining chair out, screwdriver, the door on my knee. The first screw went in stiff, and bit, and I felt it grip right up my wrist. That was the good feeling, the one my father had. Second one, third. Bosun lay in the doorway with his chin on his paws, watching. When the last went in I lifted the door on to its hinges and swung it, and it swung, and it closed, and it stayed closed. I opened it. I closed it. I stood there opening and closing it like a man with a new toy. The tins were behind it, out of sight.
