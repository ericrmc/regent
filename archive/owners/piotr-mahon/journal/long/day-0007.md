# 2019-03-10

Twenty to six, and the window was already open a hand's width. I hadn't done it. The house had gone warm in the night, and the kitchen smelled of the garden, wet earth and cut grass from somewhere. The frost was gone from the shed roof, and the roof was steaming a little where the sun had got on to it. I stood at the sink with the tea and had my sleeves pushed up. In March. It felt like being let off something.

I couldn't settle to it, though. I took the mug to the back step, came in, washed the mug, dried it and put it on the hook. Wafer sat in the patch of sun on the lino and turned over slowly, like a cake being shown to somebody. Bosun lay across the door of the hall as if to stop anyone going anywhere. I put the toast on and took it off before it was done and ate it as it was, with the lekvar going through the middle. It was the dark jar, the one from the woman at the deli. The whole day was in front of me and it didn't help. A Sunday with nothing in it is a long thing to carry about.

The bicycle was in the passage down the side of the house, where it has leaned against the wall since October. It's a Dawes, green once, and I bought it in 1998 off a lad in the dockyard for forty quid because I thought it would be good for me. I pressed my thumb on the back tyre and the thumb went in as if it were a bread roll. The front was hard enough. The back had gone down over the winter, quietly, in the dark, without a word to anyone. I found the old pump in the shed under the bootlace and the seed tin and pushed the nozzle on and worked the handle, and it made a long sigh out of the wrong end. The rubber washer had gone to biscuit. I pulled it out and it broke in two between my fingers.

I thought, market. I don't know why, because I wanted a washer, not a market. But Thursday's is the good one, with the fish and the veg and the man who sells tools off a blanket, and I couldn't wait for Thursday, and I remembered there is a Sunday one as well, on the old depot yard behind the tyre place. It is what Thursday leaves behind. I put Bosun in the back of the Octavia and drove down with the window open and my elbow out of it, which I haven't done since last summer.

It was busy. Everyone had had the same idea about the weather and had come out in the wrong clothes, the men in fleeces gone pink at the neck and the women with their coats over their arms. The yard smelled of frying onions and hot tarmac and, from somewhere behind, a mown lawn, and somebody's speaker was playing a song with a steel guitar in it. Bosun didn't like the crowd and walked pressed against my left shin the whole time, so that I kept stepping on him and saying sorry. A man was selling plants out of a wheelbarrow, mostly geraniums gone leggy from a shed. A woman had a table of jam with hand-lettered labels, and a boy of about ten sat under it eating a scotch egg out of a napkin.

The tool man was in the third row, on a folded blanket, a big man with a grey plait, sat on an upturned crate as if he'd been there since before the yard. Spanners in a row, files, a brace and bit, a tray of odd screws in a biscuit tin. I asked for a washer for a pump and he looked at me the way a doctor does. "Bike pump? What sort of pump?" I said an old one, steel, and he lifted a whole pump out of a shoebox behind him, a proper one, with a brass head and a handle worn smooth as a stair rail, and said, "Two quid. Yours for that. It's done nothing, it's only been sat." He worked the handle to show me. It made a good clean thump on the push and a sigh on the pull, and he held his palm over the nozzle and I could see the hair on his hand stand up in the little wind.

I gave him two pound coins and he wrapped it in the racing page without being asked. I could have cried, nearly, over a pump. It's a stupid thing to say. It had a weight to it I liked.

I stopped on the way out at a table of books, boxes of them, paperbacks with the covers curled in the heat, a cardboard sign saying 50p each or 3 for a pound. Sea stories, DIY, gardening, war. I don't read much. Bosun sat down on my foot. I picked up a fat blue book with no jacket, on hawks, and it opened where it had been opened most, on a page with a coffee ring. It said the bird is flown at a weight, and the weight is the control, because a bird that is not hungry does not return. I read it standing. There was a diagram of a set of scales next to it and a table with weights in ounces. The man at the table was reading the racing at the other end and didn't look up. I put the book back in its box, face down, the way I found it, and I don't know why I was careful about that.

The pump went on the tyre in the passage at half past nine. Brass head on the valve, and I did it slowly, forty strokes, with the bike up on its saddle and handlebars on the flags and the wheel ticking round when I'd done. It came up fat and tight. I pressed it with my thumb and the thumb stayed on top, the way it does on a good loaf. I stood there feeling pleased, which was out of all proportion. Two pounds. A man at a market had put a pump in my hand and it worked.

That was the trouble. It was half nine and I'd done the thing I got up to do, and there was the whole of the day sat behind it.

So I went round for the loppers. I put the tape in my back pocket first, and took it out again, and put it back in the drawer by the string. Then I got it once more and put it in the coat on the hook. I don't know what I thought I was going to do with it at Barry's front door.

He was out on his drive with the hose and a bucket, washing the Rover, in a vest, his arms very white above the elbow and brown below like a man in sleeves. The Rover was already clean. He was doing the wheel arches with a brush the size of a hand. The sun was on the roof of it and there were rainbows in the suds going down the drive.

"Piotr," he said, and straightened up with a hand on his back. "Lovely day for it."

"It is." I stood at the end of his path. I could see the side of his shed over his shoulder, the door open a foot, and a dark in there. "I'm after my loppers, Barry. The long ones. You had them for the top of the hedge."

"Loppers." He said it like a word from a language he'd had at school. He put the brush in the bucket. "The green handles."

"That's them."

"I had them, yes. Now." He looked at the shed and then at the sky, and I saw him go along the shelves in his head. "I did give them to Roy. Roy's got that apple tree, you see, he'd have done the lot with the saw but the branch was that high. He said a week. I'll have a word."

"Roy your brother-in-law."

"Roy, yes, at Elburton."

I said all right. I said it in the voice for the newly bereaved, the level one, and I heard it come out of me and didn't much like it. Barry wrung out the chamois and looked up the road and back at me, and there was a space where either of us could have said something about a fence, eighteen inches, a man called Trewin. I could feel the letter under the pig from here. It was a hundred yards off and on my mind like a hand.

"Lovely day," Barry said again.

"Yes."

"They say it's back to cold Wednesday."

The hose was still running. It made a small bright noise on the drive, running down to the gutter, and I stood and listened to it for a long time. Then I said I'd let him get on, and he said, "Lovely," a third time, and lifted the brush to me like a man saluting from a ship.

I walked back the long way, round by the corner, to have the sun on my face. The whole street had its doors open. Somebody had a radio on in an upstairs room, and a smell of roast came out of one house and bacon out of the next. A girl of about six went by on a scooter with her father running after and shouting, and the wheels on the pavement were a dry hard clatter, and everything was too loud and too bright, as if it were all laid on for somebody who wasn't me.

I took the bike out at eleven. I don't know when I last did that. It sat very odd under me, the saddle too low, my knees up, and the chain gave a groan I could have told from the road. But the tyre held. I went down Outland Road and across the top and out along the flat past the allotments, where the gates were all open and the sheds had their doors back, men in there with kettles. Then I went further than I meant to. My legs remembered it better than I did. The air off the water came up at me cool and salt in warm patches, and my breath went short and ragged by the second hill, and I got off and pushed, and there was a moment at the top when I stood with the bike against my hip and the whole of the Sound was laid out below, flat and glittering, with a ship on the horizon that wasn't moving, that had all day.

I thought about phoning Roisin. I actually took the phone out of my coat and held it. It was a Sunday, she'd be in, and I'd said I'd get a card and I hadn't, and the last I'd heard of her was a phone put down on a hard table and a door a long way off. I looked at the screen. It said 11.52 and the picture was the dog. I put it away again and looked at the ship.

On the way down I let the bike run. The wind went through my shirt and my eyes ran, and I could hear the back tyre on the road, a light steady hum, a good sound, and I kept my fingers over the brakes and didn't touch them, all the way to the bottom, and at the bottom I stood on the pedals for a second to see if I still could, and I could, and the chain went with a crack like a shot.

I got off and looked at it. It hung from the back wheel in a loop, one link bent open, and I knelt on the verge with it in my hands, black to the wrist, and there were still four miles home. I hadn't brought the pump, or the tape, and I thought of Barry's loppers hanging on a nail in Elburton, or not, and I began to laugh, or something like it, and a woman going by with a spaniel gave me a wide berth, and I said, "It's all right, love, it's only the chain," and she said, "I'm sure it is," and went on. The spaniel looked back at me twice.
