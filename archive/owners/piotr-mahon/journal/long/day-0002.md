Slept through until twenty to six, which I don't trust. Lay there waiting to see what it had cost me and it hadn't cost anything. Tea in the Argyle mug. The window over the sink was clear for once, no rain and no face in it, just the garden going from black to grey to a hard pale blue, and a frost on the shed roof that made it look like someone had iced it. The sky was empty. Cold enough that the cat flap had a rim of white round it.

The cupboard by the cooker was open, of course. I have stopped taking it personally. But I had a clear head, so I got the screwdriver out of the drawer and took the door right off, which it was nearly ready to do anyway, and had a proper look. The screw holes in the chipboard have gone to crumbs. You could put a finger in one. So I did the thing my father did with anything that had lost its grip, which was matchsticks and glue. Snapped the heads off six Swan Vestas, dipped them in the wood glue, pushed them into the holes with the flat of the screwdriver until they were tight, and cut the ends flush with a Stanley knife. It smelled of the glue and a little of sulphur. Con used to say you'd never get a screw to hold in a hole that had given up unless you gave it something to argue with. I set the door on the worktop and told myself twenty-four hours before I touched it. The shelf inside looked very exposed, the tins and the foil and the saucepan all showing, like a mouth with the lip pulled back.

Wafer came and looked at it, then at me. She wanted her food, which was in there. I got the packet out over the top and she stood on the rim of the bowl and asked anyway.

At ten to eight the noise started next door. A hedge trimmer, petrol, that high stuttering whine that goes up and down as he leans on it. Barry in his own garden with the sun on him, cutting the leylandii. I stood in the kitchen with the cat food in one hand and listened to him work along the top, and I could tell where he was by the pitch. It came and went and came back. Bits of green were flying up over the fence. A few landed on the lawn on my side, small and bright, like something spilled. I thought, he's doing it now so I'll see, or he's doing it now because it's the first dry morning in a fortnight and that's all. Both of those could be true. I don't know which is worse.

The dog was at my knee. He doesn't like the trimmer. He went and stood in the hall, in the worn place, with his ears back, and I gave him a biscuit and told him nobody was coming for him. Pilsudski, on the third stair, didn't move a whisker.

The compost was in the hall too. I'd got it on Sunday at the garden place in Crownhill, fifty litres of the peat-free that Reg says is no good and I bought anyway. I'd propped it against the wall behind the front door so it wouldn't get wet, meaning to take it up to the allotment tonight. When I went to lift it, the whole side let go. The seam had been going all along, I suppose, and I put my hand under it in the wrong place. A slump of dark crumb ran out over the tiles, and onto the mat, and some of it into the dog's water. It was damp and cold and smelled of a wood floor after rain. It got under my nails at once.

I stood there for a bit. It is a stupid thing to feel put out about, a bag of compost. I got the dustpan, which lives in the cupboard, or did, and found it on the floor of the bathroom for reasons I can't account for, and I swept what I could back into the bag, and then I couldn't stand the bag up, so I put it in the big blue crate the cats' litter came in, and it sat there with its split side turned to the wall. There was still a stripe of it across the tiles. Bosun looked at it, and at me, and stepped over it, high, like a man crossing a stream.

The phone went at twenty past eight. The vet on the Plain. A girl, young. She said, "Hi, is that Mr Mahon?" and I said "Mahn," and she said "Sorry, Mr Mahn," and I let it go. It was about Bosun's teeth. He was booked for Thursday at four, the check, and she said the vet had a thing come in and could I do Monday at eleven instead. I said I'd have to see about Monday. She said no problem, and that they'd hold it until Friday. I said I would have a look at the rota. There was a pause on the line and then she said, "Only he'll probably want to quote you for a scale and polish, so it's worth—" and then the line made a noise like something tearing and went. I stood there with it against my ear. I could have rung back. I put the phone on the table by the letter. I thought, if it's important she'll ring, and then I thought, that's what people say when they don't want to know.

A text at nine, when I was already in the Octavia. It buzzed against my thigh at the lights on Mutley Plain and I read it at the next red. Roisin. "Don't worry about Thursday for the gutters, Pete, someone's looking at it. Thanks anyway x." She's the only one who calls me Pete. I read it twice. I had the ladder on the roof rack from last time, still strapped, and I looked in the mirror at it, the two ends sticking out. Someone's looking at it. Someone. The lights changed and a van behind me leaned on the horn, and I pulled away. It's a gutter. I know what's in that gutter, there's a buddleia in it. I'd have been up there an hour and had a tea at the end.

At Voisey's it was the yard, the hearse first. The light was low and hard and white, and it showed everything, every swirl the chamois leaves in the paint, every water mark on the wheel arches. On a grey day you'd never see it. I did the roof with the long brush and the door shuts with a cloth, and my hands went stiff and red with the cold water. Marta came out at ten with a mug and said, "That's lovely, that," of the bonnet, and I said it was only water. She looked at the sky and said, "Don't get used to it," and went back in, and that's the whole conversation we had.

I did the limousines after. The second one has a rattle in the back door, something loose in the card, and I spent a good while with my ear against it, knocking with the flat of my hand, trying to place it. Ten past twelve and I still hadn't found it. The frost had gone off everything but the shaded side of the wall, where it stayed in a long white strip along the bottom of the bricks.

I ate the sausage roll in the stairwell, on the middle landing, under the strip light that ticks. It was warm from the bag and the pastry flaked down my front. Someone came down and past me and said "All right," and I said "All right," and they went on. I looked at my phone and Roisin's message was still on it. I typed "No problem" and then took it out and typed "Fine" and then took that out too. In the end I put a thumb up, which I never do. It looked like something a stranger had sent.

Afternoon was light. A family in the front office, an old man, seventy-odd, in a coat he must have bought for a different sort of day, both hands on his knees. He had come about his wife. He kept saying "She'd have wanted it done properly," and nobody had asked him what she'd have wanted. Marta looked at me over his head and I went and got the chair, the good one with arms, from the corner, and set it a little behind him, so that when he was ready he'd sit. He didn't yet. He stood and talked, and I stood, and the chair waited. It was nearly ten minutes. He looked down, then, at my shoes, and said, "You keep them nice." I said they were my father's last, and that wasn't true, but it was a thing to say.

Home at half five. The sun going down orange over the roofs opposite and the whole street lit sideways like a stage. Cold in the car already. Barry's hedge, when I went out the back and stood at the top of the garden, was a good foot lower and flat as a table, and where it had been there was a gap, and through the gap the fence, plain to see, the whole length of it, the green bottoms and the leaning posts. Bare. He'd cut it back to the panels. It looked much smaller than it had. I stood there with my coat on and the cold coming up through my soles. Somewhere behind the wall a radio was on, low, a man reading the shipping forecast, or the end of it, and I couldn't make out the sea area.

The cupboard door was on the worktop where I'd left it. I went to test one of the matchsticks with my thumbnail and stopped, because it was too soon. Wafer sat under the bread bin, looking up at it.
