Twenty to six, and the window over the sink was ticking. Sleet, small and hard, then a gap, then rain, so that I couldn't tell by the sound which one I was listening to. Tea, thumb on the chip. The cupboard under the sink was open its finger's width. The beer mat was still on the lino where I had left it yesterday, and this morning I stepped over it. I am not proud of that. I am also not going to pick it up on a Sunday just because the cupboard wants me to.

Bosun's blanket had to go. Pilsudski was sick on it in the night, and the machine in the kitchen has been leaking from the door seal since the week before last, a slow brown weep on the lino that I mop and then forget. So I stuffed the blanket and the dog's bed cover and two of my work shirts into a bin bag and took them to the launderette on the Plain, a bag over each shoulder, with the sleet coming sideways off the Co-op roof and stinging the good side of my neck.

It is a place I like more than I ought to. The windows are steamed to the top and someone has written a name on the inside with a finger, KELLY, and let it run. The floor is that red lino that has been mopped so many times it has gone pale in the walking lines. The heat hits you at the door, and the smell, hot lint and powder, and something under it like a warm iron. Two machines going. A woman in a dressing gown under a mac sat with her arms folded watching hers turn, as if it might try something. I put my things in the big one at the end, because the blanket is thick, and fed it the coins. The slot takes the pound coins only if you push them flat with your thumb. It's been like that since I first came here, when I was with Roisin and the machine at Stoke had broken and she'd sent me down with a duvet. I don't think about that, and I thought about it.

The man came in about ten past seven, or rather the man's voice did, before the door had shut behind him, talking to somebody outside on the pavement who was evidently still there. Mid thirties, a hi-vis jacket over a hooded top, wet through at the shoulders. He propped the door with his foot and went on talking. "No, my brother, right, he does bridges. Inspects them. For the council, or it was, now it's some firm. And he says you don't look at 'em, or you do, but that's not the job. He's got this little hammer and he goes along the steel tapping it, and he listens. That's it. And if the note's dull, if it's gone flat, that's a hole. Something's gone behind it. You could stand there all day looking at it and it'd be perfect, you know? Paint's on, no rust, nothing. But it goes dull." The other voice said something I didn't catch. "Yeah," he said. "Well. He says you get so you can hear it before you've thought about it." He let the door go and it banged, and he shook himself like a dog and went to the machines with a laundry bag in his arms, and did not say another word to anyone.

I watched my blanket go round. There was a bicycle chained to the drainpipe outside, and I could see it through the clear place in the steam, a woman's bike, dark green, with a basket. Its back tyre was flat as a pancake, the wheel resting on its rim in the wet. The man in the hi-vis went out to it when his wash was on, crouched, pressed the tyre with his thumb, and stood up with his hands on his hips. I went out. I don't know why. "Slow puncture?" I said. He said it was his mum's, he'd brought it down to fix and forgotten the patches. I have a pump in the Octavia. It's a long brass thing, my father's, that was in the rigging shed at the dockyard for God knows how many years. I got it out of the boot in the sleet and pumped, and he held the wheel, and the tyre came up hard and round under my hands. "That'll go down again," he said. I said probably. I pumped it a good deal harder than it needed. "You'll blow it," he said. I said I wouldn't. He looked at me, and at the tyre, and said, "Cheers, mate," and did not ask me anything else, and I liked him for that. When I went back in I could hear the air hissing very faintly, or thought I could, and decided I couldn't.

The phone went in my pocket while I was folding the shirts, the cracked one, the glass catching my thumb. A text, from the number at Derriford. It said my appointment on the second had been rescheduled owing to a clinic change, and gave me a new one, the Thursday, ten past two, and told me to reply YES to confirm or call to discuss. I read it standing at the folding table with a wet-warm shirt over my arm. Thursday at ten past two I have a chapel to set and a family coming at four. The Tuesday was fixed. The Tuesday was on paper, in the envelope, under my hand on Level 6 with the hail coming down. I put the phone away. I didn't reply YES. I thought I would go on the Tuesday anyway, with the letter, and stand at the desk, and let them tell me to my face that they didn't have me. It is not a good plan and I like it.

The blanket came out hot and heavy, and I carried it home against my chest like a child, and it steamed under my chin the whole way up Outland Road.

At one I put on the black suit. Marta had asked on Friday if I'd go along to the wake at the Naval club, Devonport, for the woman we buried yesterday, Mrs Tregear, ninety-one, four sons in matching ties. Not for the food. The family had ordered the flowers to be taken on to the club, and someone had to carry the boards in and check the cards had not got wet, and see the lot back in the van when they'd done. Kieran was going too. I said all right. I said it before she had finished the sentence.

The function room was upstairs, over the bar, with a carpet with anchors on it and a hatch through which two women passed plates of sandwiches cut in triangles. The sleet was back. It hit the long windows and slid. The boards went against the wall under a picture of the Ark Royal, and I touched the cards, and they were dry. The four sons had all had two pints by the time I got there. One of them took my hand in both of his and said he wanted me to know that his mother had loved the way we had done her hair, and I said that was kind, and he said, "She'd never had it like that," and cried, quite suddenly, with his mouth open, and a brother came and turned him round by the shoulders. I brought him a chair. He did not sit in it. He held the back of it as if it might get away.

Kieran was going round with a tray of teas. He had taken his jacket off and rolled his cuffs back a turn, which Marta would have something to say about. He is quick. He doesn't spill. He came to me with a cup and said, "Piotr, you want sugar?" and I said no, and he said, "I'll put one in anyway, you look like you need it," and did, and stirred it with a small spoon he had ready in his top pocket. He smiled. I took the cup.

The widow's sister, or the sister of the woman we'd buried, I lost track, sat by the window in a grey wool coat she did not take off. She had her hands in her lap, and she was turning a ring on her left hand round and round with her right thumb, a thin gold one worn nearly through at the back. She turned it all the while she talked, to nobody, to the window. "They said take it off her," she said to me, when I had stood beside her long enough. "I said no, she'll want it. Dorothy'll want it. Forty years she had it on." I said that the family would have chosen. She said, "Did she go with it?" I said, "She did." I have said that a hundred times and it has always been true. I heard it come out of my mouth and it was the same as always, level, and I stood there with the cup in my hand and could not have said what I was looking at. Across the room Kieran was bending to a small boy with a plate. He put the plate down on a chair for him, and straightened up, and put his hand in his trouser pocket, and I looked at the picture of the Ark Royal until he took it out again with only a handkerchief in it.

It is nothing. It is the ordinary thing, a lad with a handkerchief. I have seen him do nothing else for weeks, and do it well, and I say to myself, what did you see, and I see it again, on the stairs, the middle landing, the light going, and I put it away again in the same place. I have told nobody. There is no one to tell who would not have to do something about it, and I do not know that I want it done. That is the thing I do not say aloud even in the house with the dog.

"He's a good lad," said the sister in the grey coat, following my eyes. "Very nice manner." I said he was.

We loaded the van in the dark, at twenty to five. Kieran took the heavy end without being asked, and talked the whole time, about football, about his girlfriend's mother's caravan in Looe, about a video he'd seen of a man being chased by a swan. I said yes, and did he. The sleet had turned to rain again and ran down the back of my collar. When we'd shut the doors he stood there a moment with the rain on his face and said, "Marta said you might show me the prep room properly, sometime. Not the tidying. Proper." I said she had mentioned it. "No rush," he said. "Only if you want." I said I would see, and got in the cab, and he stood at the passenger door for a second before he understood he was meant to walk round.

At home the blanket was still on the radiator, dry and stiff and smelling of the launderette, and Bosun was lying on it with his chin on his paws. He didn't get up. Pilsudski was on the third stair.
