Twenty to six, and there was no window. There was a grey square where the window went, and nothing behind it. I couldn't see the fence or the hedge, or even the top of the shed roof, which I can see on the worst nights. After yesterday's clear frost it felt like being handed back the wrong coat. Bosun was across my shins. I lay with my left ear down and listened for the pipes, and they didn't knock. The whole house had that wadded sound, as if somebody had put a blanket over it.

It was Sunday, so no drill. I noticed the quiet in the plaster before I noticed anything else. I went down in my socks, and Pilsudski on the third stair opened one eye, looked at me, looked at Bosun coming behind, and shut it again. Bosun stood on the fourth stair with his front feet together and waited to be carried. I carried him. It's a Sunday kind of thing to do, with nobody about.

The cupboard door was on the kitchen table where I'd left it overnight, with the dowels sticking up out of the hinge hole like three pale teeth. I snapped them flush with the back of a knife. They'd set hard. The yellow glue had come out round the edges in a rubbery little collar and I peeled it off in one piece and rolled it between finger and thumb. I didn't put the screw in yet. Barry had said tomorrow, and it was tomorrow, but I stood there with the driver in my hand and thought I'd do it when I could see properly. The light in the kitchen was the colour of dishwater and didn't move.

The tea was the trouble. I'd filled the green flask at six, the old one with the cork in the cup and a dent at the base, and I poured a cup at half past seven to have with the toast. It came out warm. Not hot, only warm, like something that had been held. I put my hand round the outside and the steel was cold as the tap, and I thought, well, it's not keeping anything in. I unscrewed the stopper and looked down at the silver and shook it by my ear. Something rattled. A small loose sound, like a tooth in a matchbox. That's the glass gone, the vacuum. I'd known it for a year, and I've been filling it every day and pretending. I stood with it in both hands. I tried the stopper the other way up, and turned the whole thing over on the drainer to see if it would tell me anything. It wouldn't. I put it by the bin and then took it out again and set it on the windowsill next to the Courage beer mat and the cup with the teaspoon still in it. That's a whole shelf of things I can't throw away. I made the tea again in the Argyle mug and drank it too fast and burned my tongue on it, on principle.

The phone was on the wall in the hall, by the leaning place where the cupboard door had been. I looked at it more than I wanted to. Four o'clock, I'd said to myself yesterday, and I hadn't rung at four, because at four I'd been sitting with my hands either side of a door. It was a clean reason and I hadn't thought it through. I had a feeling now that if I stayed in the house the phone would ring or wouldn't ring, and either way I'd be standing in the hall with my head on one side. So at nine I put on the old donkey jacket and the boots, and clipped Bosun's lead, and went out.

I walked. I'd no plan for it. The fog was down on the road so that the parked cars were only shapes with wet shoulders, and every streetlamp still had its halo on and nothing to shine at. I went down Outland Road and across, and along by the park where the railings bled orange onto the pavement, and I could hear trains somewhere, or thought I could. Bosun kept close, nose to my calf. A man went by with a newspaper folded under his arm and said, "Filthy," and I said it was, and that was the whole of the conversation for a mile.

I went down through Mutley and past the deli, shuttered, with a bit of card in the door about the lekvar that I couldn't read in that light. Then I found I'd gone on and on, toward the Hoe, toward where the sea should have been. It wasn't. At the wall there was a white blank with a foghorn somewhere in it, one long note and then a gap. I counted the gap. Nineteen. Then it went again. I stood there with the dog sitting on my boot and nothing to look at, and I thought, this is what a man does who's got a phone he doesn't want to answer. I thought it quite calmly. Then I turned round.

By noon I was at the yard. I hadn't meant to be. My feet took me the back way, up the lane behind Mutley Plain, and there were the gates with the chain looped through, and my key on the ring, and Bosun already wagging at the smell of the place, which is soap and diesel and something under it. There was nobody in. I let us in at the side and pulled the fire door hard with my shoulder, where it sticks, and it gave with the noise it always makes, and we were in the stairwell.

It's a different room on a Sunday. The strip light on the middle landing wasn't on, and the fog came in a bit through the frosted glass so the cream walls looked wet. The rubber treads smelled of rain. Somebody's mop bucket stood at the foot with a grey skin on it. I sat on the second step with my back to the rail and Bosun sat on my feet, and I got out the sausage roll from the Greggs at the bottom of Mutley, which was cold and greasy and very good. Nobody was hoovering. I could hear the prep room fan through the door on its low setting, and that was all. I ate it looking at the green treads going up into the dark and thought about nothing, which is harder than it sounds. My left ear rang a little. I've noticed that lately in shut places, and I do nothing about it.

The thermos was in my pocket. I'd brought it after all, without thinking, with the last of the morning's tea. I poured a bit in the cap. Cold as a church, and stewed. I drank it anyway. I've drunk worse on that step, in January, from the machine.

It was good to be somewhere that was mine, in a way, and had no expectations of me. I had my hand on the rail, worn shiny by forty years of shoulders, and I could feel a small dip in it about where I always put my thumb. I wondered who'd made the dip first. It would be a nice thing to ask Nula. I didn't finish that. I pushed the cap back on and stood up, and Bosun stood up, and we went out the way we'd come and I looped the chain, and the fog had thickened if anything. I could hardly see the end of the lane.

I got home at three. The phone hadn't rung, or if it had it had done it while I was out. I hung the lead on its hook and stood in the hall and did not look at the phone. I made myself do it. Then I went in and got the door up on the table and put a screw in the first dowel, slowly, feeling for the grip. It went in and bit. It went in another turn and bit again, and I thought, that's it, that's held. The next turn went round and round and gave nothing, like a screw in cake. I backed it out. There was white dust on the thread.
